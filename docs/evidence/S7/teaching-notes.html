On the record

Your predictions, before any code ran

Setup as posed: you hold one European call on a stock that follows 2.6's Geometric Brownian motion (GBM), dS = μS dt + σS dW, and you short Δ = ∂V/∂S shares against it, resetting the share position N times over the option's life; the cash difference sits in an account at the riskless rate r. V(t, S) is the option's value, taken from the closed form as a black box. "Realised vol" is the σ the stock actually moves with; "implied vol" is the σ the hedger prices and hedges at. The forward, hedged the same way with Δ = 1, is the control (your setup ruling: the call's V and Δ from the formula 2.9 derives). Gut answers, not derivations.

P1
Hold one call, short Δ = ∂V/∂S shares, rebalance N times, realised vol = implied vol. Is the final P&L exactly zero, zero on average with a spread, or biased? If a spread, how does it shrink with N — 1/N, 1/√N, not at all?
You: 0 on average with a spread which shrinks like 1/√N  right, both — §3: mean 0 within s.e. from N = 64, sd 6.50 → 0.22 over N = 4 → 4096, slope −0.49.
P2
Same hedge, but the stock drifts up strongly (μ = 0.3) instead of μ = 0. Does the hedged P&L's mean change? Does the unhedged option's P&L mean change?
You: yes and yes  hedged: no — within s.e. from N = 64 on (at N = 4 a trace of +0.7 on a spread of 7, gone by N = 64); unhedged: yes — the mean moves from −3.1 to +22.1 — §4. μ vanishes from the hedged book, not from the stock.
P3
Realised vol 0.5 against an implied 0.4, hedged at the implied delta. Is the hedger's P&L positive, negative or zero on average? What quantity is it proportional to?
You: zero, proportional to implied vol  positive: +3.74 ± 0.03 on an 18.02 option; ∝ (σ²_realised − σ²_implied) × Σ ½ΓS² dt, not the implied vol — §5. The hedger is paid for the gamma the stock delivers above what the price assumed.
P4
The forward (linear payoff) hedged the same way: what is its P&L spread, and does it depend on N?
You: spread 0, does not depend on N  right, both — §3: |P&L| ≤ 4 × 10⁻¹¹ on every path at every N, for every μ and σ. Δ = 1 is exact for a linear payoff.
P5
In dΠ = dV − Δ dS, which term cancels when Δ = ∂V/∂S, and what is left — write it, drift first.
You: dS cancels, leaving dV − dV = 0  the ∂V/∂S·dS term cancels — right, and the dW and μ go with it; what is left is not 0: (∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S²) dt, and the PDE is the statement that this equals r(V − ΔS) dt — bridge rung 5, §6. If dΠ were 0 identically, P1's spread would be zero at N = 1.

P1 and P4 are the page's mechanics and you had both. P2, P3 and P5 are the three things the page exists to show, and they are one fact seen three ways: the hedge removes the dW term and μ with it (P2), what remains is the gamma term and it is not zero (P5), and when the stock's σ is not the price's σ that remainder is what the hedger earns (P3). P5's "dV − dV" and P1's "spread" contradict each other, which is the fastest way to see why dΠ ≠ 0.

The so what

2Three decisions a hedger makes with this page

A desk that sells an option does not want its P&L to depend on where the stock goes; it holds shares against the option so that it does not. The PDE is the statement that this can be done perfectly in the limit; the simulator is what tells a developer how close a real hedge gets and what the gaps cost. Three decisions, each with a wrong answer that produces a plausible-looking number.

"Rung" below means a step of the derivation, laid out as a seven-rung ladder in the bridge after §5 (theory after numbers). In one line each: 1 2.6's dV line; 2 the portfolio Π = V − ΔS, Δ held fixed over a step; 3 substitute dS and collect; 4 choose Δ = ∂V/∂S, which removes dW and μ together; 5 riskless must earn r; 6 the PDE; 7 what a discrete hedge is left with per step, ½ΓS²((δS/S)² − σ²dt). Every "rung n" in the notes is a link to it.

1 · How often do you rebalance?
Every rebalance costs something; what does it buy? One static hedge takes the unhedged spread of 29.6 to 11.8 — the linear exposure, removed once. From there the P&L spread falls like 1/√N: 4× the rebalances halves it, 6.50 at N = 4, 0.22 at N = 4096 on an 18.02 option. The wrong answers are "hedging makes the P&L zero" and "the spread is 1/N". §3.
2 · What do you do about the drift?
Nothing — once you rebalance. μ cancels with the dW term, so a hedger does not need a view on it and does not get paid for one: μ = −0.2, 0, +0.3 give the same hedged mean and spread within s.e. from 64 rebalances on, while the unhedged mean swings from −11.7 to +22.1. The wrong answer is "a short share position bleeds when the stock rallies". §4.
3 · Which volatility do you hedge at — and what happens if it is wrong?
The price and the delta are built from one σ; the stock moves with another. Hedged at 0.4 while the stock moves at 0.5, the hedger earns +3.74 on average, at 0.3 loses 3.76, and the number is (σ²_r − σ²_i) times the gamma the path delivered. That P&L keeps a spread of 1.3–1.7 however often you hedge, because the gamma integral is path-dependent. The wrong answers are "zero — the hedge removes all risk" and "proportional to the vol itself". §5.

The maths the page needs is six rungs long (the bridge, after §5) and 2.5 did five of them; what is new is "hold Δ fixed over the step" and "riskless earns r". What is new in the numbers is rung 7: the hedger's P&L is 2.5's (dW)² − dt noise with a price on it, and the three decisions are three readings of that one line — its spread (decision 1), the terms it does not contain (decision 2), and its mean (decision 3).

This is the sentence the course is built on, in one table column.

The bridge — from 2.6's last paragraph to 2.7's result

From dV to the PDE, one move at a time

§3–§5 measured three readings of one line; this is where the line comes from. 2.6 ended by applying the general form to V(t, S) and promising that choosing Δ kills the dW term. Every rung below adds one thing to the rung before it and names where that thing was already shown. Say which rung is the first you do not recognise.

  1. 2.6 "Into 2.7" — 2.5's general form on V(t, S), a = μS, b = σSdV = (∂V/∂t + μS·∂V/∂S + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S²) dt + σS·∂V/∂S dWThe first function of both t and S. ∂V/∂t is new: the option loses or gains value as time passes with S fixed. The ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S² is 2.5's ½b²·∂²f/∂x² with b = σS, as on 2.6's log S, but now ∂²V/∂S² is not −1/S² and nothing cancels.
  2. New — the portfolio, and the one rule about itΠ = V − ΔS, dΠ = dV − Δ dS — Δ held fixed over the stepHold the option and short Δ shares. Over one step the shares are whatever you held at its start, so their P&L is Δ times the price move and nothing else — there is no dΔ·S term. That is what "rebalance" means: Δ changes between steps, at prices you pay. §6 checks this bookkeeping to 10⁻¹² per path.
  3. Rung 1 and 2, with 2.6's dS = μS dt + σS dW substituteddΠ = (∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S²) dt + (∂V/∂S − Δ)·μS dt + (∂V/∂S − Δ)·σS dWCollect the dt terms and the dW terms. Both the drift and the noise of the stock enter only through the same factor, (∂V/∂S − Δ).
  4. New — the choiceΔ = ∂V/∂S ⇒ dΠ = (∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S²) dtOne choice removes the dW term and the μ term, because they shared a factor. What is left has no dW, so over the next instant Π moves by a known amount: the portfolio is locally riskless. This is P5's answer, and it is not zero.
  5. New — a riskless portfolio must earn the riskless ratedΠ = rΠ dt = r(V − ΔS) dtMoney tied up in Π is V − ΔS (you paid V for the option and received ΔS for the shares). If a riskless position earned more than r you would borrow at r and hold it; if less, you would short it and lend. So its drift is r, whatever μ is. That is the only place r enters, and the only place "no arbitrage" is used.
  6. Rung 4 and 5 equated — the Black–Scholes PDE∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S² + rS·∂V/∂S − rV = 0Divide nothing, just move everything to one side. A PDE in (t, S) with the payoff max(S_T − K, 0) as its condition at t = T. μ is not in it; r and σ are. 2.9 writes down the function that solves it; §6 checks that function against this line to 10⁻¹⁵.
  7. Q1

You hold a call and short Δ = ∂V/∂S shares, rebalancing N times; the stock moves at the σ the price assumed. The final P&L is:

  • Zero on average, with a spread that falls like 1/√N — sd 6.50, 3.41, 1.72, 0.87, 0.44, 0.22 at N = 4 … 4096 (slope −0.49); 4× the rebalances halves it. The spread is Σ ½ΓS²((δS/S)² − σ²dt), 2.5's (dW)² − dt noise.
  • Exactly zero: the hedge removes all risk — continuous hedging assumed on a discrete grid. dΠ = 0 holds only in the limit; on a grid (δS)² is not σ²S²dt step by step. At N = 4 the sd is 6.50 on an 18.02 option.
  • Zero on average, with a spread that falls like 1/N — the error assumed dt-sized. N terms of mean zero and sd ∝ dt add to √N·dt = 1/√N. Measured: 64× the rebalances cuts the spread 8×, not 64×.
  • Biased, because the short shares lose when the stock rises — the hedge mistaken for a bet on direction. The option gains what the shares lose to first order; the mean is 0 within s.e. from N = 64, and the same at μ = +0.3.
Q2

Same hedge, but the stock now drifts at μ = +0.3 instead of 0. What changes?

  • The hedged P&L's mean and spread do not change within s.e.; the unhedged option's mean rises from −3.1 to +22.1 — μ enters dΠ only through (∂V/∂S − Δ)·μS dt, which Δ = ∂V/∂S removes. Hedged sd 0.223 vs 0.209 at N = 4096; unhedged sd 29.6 → 51.5.
  • Both means rise: a rising stock helps the call either way — the hedge thought to follow the stock. The hedged book holds the call and is short Δ shares; the shares give back what the call gains. Hedged mean −0.002 ± 0.003 at μ = 0.3, N = 4096.
  • The hedged P&L falls: you are short shares in a rally — the short leg read on its own. Short Δ shares lose Δ·δS; the call gains ∂V/∂S·δS, the same number. What is left is ½Γ(δS)², which has no sign from μ.
  • The unhedged mean does not change, because the option's price does not depend on μ — price and P&L confused. The price (set by the PDE) ignores μ; the payoff's expectation under the real-world drift does not: E[(S_T − K)⁺] is 15.85 at μ = 0 and 41.0 at μ = 0.3.
Q3

You price and hedge at an implied vol of 0.4; the stock turns out to move at 0.5. Your hedged P&L, on average, is:

  • Positive, proportional to σ²_realised − σ²_implied times the gamma delivered along the path — +3.74 ± 0.03 on an 18.02 option at N = 4096, against (0.25 − 0.16) × Σ e^(−rt)·½ΓS²dt = 0.09 × 41.57 = 3.74 on the same paths. 2.10's "theta pays for gamma".
  • Zero: the delta hedge has removed the risk — direction and activity confused. Δ removes the stock's direction (the dW and μ terms). Its activity, (δS)², stays, and the price only paid for σ² = 0.16 of it per unit time; the stock delivered 0.25.
  • Negative: hedging at too low a vol loses money — the sign read from the price, not the gamma. A long option is long gamma: it gains ½Γ(δS)² on every move and pays Θ dt for it; a stock that moves more than the price assumed pays more than it costs. At σ_r = 0.3 the sign flips: −3.76.
  • Positive, proportional to the implied vol — the premium mistaken for the P&L. The implied vol sets what you paid; the P&L is the difference between what the stock delivered and what you paid for, and it is zero when σ_r = σ_i whatever σ_i is (0.00 ± 0.004 at 0.4).
Q4

You hedge a forward contract, V = S − Ke^(−rτ), the same way: short Δ = ∂V/∂S = 1 share per forward, rebalancing N times. Its P&L is:

  • Zero on every path at every N, to rounding — |P&L| ≤ 4 × 10⁻¹¹ across 4,000 paths × 6 grids × 5 scenarios. Γ = 0, so there is no ½Γ(δS)² term and nothing for a rebalance to correct: a linear payoff is hedged once, exactly. The course's thesis in one row.
  • Zero on average with a 1/√N spread, like the call — every derivative assumed to need frequent rebalancing. The spread comes from Γ, and the forward has none. Δ = 1 is right at every S and every t, so the hedge is never stale.
  • Zero on average with a spread that depends on μ — the drift put back into the hedged book. With Δ = 1 the shares cancel the forward's exposure to S exactly, μ included; the cash leg earns r on Ke^(−rτ) and delivers K at T.
  • Not zero: the forward has no premium, so there is nothing to earn r on — the cash leg forgotten. Shorting the share brings in S₀; the forward costs S₀ − Ke^(−rT); the difference Ke^(−rT) sits in cash and grows to exactly K, the amount owed at delivery.
Q5

Π = V − ΔS with Δ = ∂V/∂S, and dS = μS dt + σS dW. Which term cancels in dΠ, and what is left?

  • The dW term cancels, and the μ term with it (they share the factor ∂V/∂S − Δ); what is left is (∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S²) dt, and the PDE says this equals r(V − ΔS) dt — checked on the closed form to 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ and at every point 24,000 hedges visited.
  • Everything cancels: dΠ = dV − dV = 0 — the second-order term dropped. Δ dS matches only the ∂V/∂S·dS part of dV; the ∂V/∂t dt and ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S² dt parts remain. If dΠ were 0 the hedged P&L would be exactly zero on a grid of 1; it is 6.50 wide at N = 4.
  • The dt term cancels, leaving pure noise — the roles of Δ swapped. Δ multiplies dS, and dS carries the dW; choosing Δ = ∂V/∂S is what removes the dW. The dt term is what no choice of Δ can touch, which is why it must be r Π dt.
  • The dW term cancels but μS·∂V/∂S dt survives, so μ is in the PDE — the shares' drift forgotten. The short shares have drift −Δ·μS dt, and with Δ = ∂V/∂S it is the same size as the option's μS·∂V/∂S dt. μ is absent from the PDE and the checks show it absent from the hedged P&L.
