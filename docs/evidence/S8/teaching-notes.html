Before anything else

What this page is about, and what it is trying to do

In one sentence: this page is about how to compute an option price now that 2.7 has told you what the price must satisfy, and why the method every desk uses looks wrong and isn't.

2.7 ended with a differential equation and no μ in it. A PDE is one way to get a number out (solve it on a grid, which is 3.3). This page gives the other way, the one you would actually write first: simulate the stock many times, average the payoff, discount. That pricer has one slot you have to fill — the drift — and the natural thing to put in it, the stock's expected return, gives the wrong answer. Put r in, the rate the stock certainly does not earn, and you get the right one. The page's job is to make a developer confident in three things: that this is the correct recipe (drift r, and report the standard error, because a Monte Carlo price is a price ± something); that it is not a trick — the reason is 2.7's hedge, which earns r whatever the stock does, so the price has to be the average under the one drift that makes the discounted stock a fair game; and that "switching to the risk-neutral measure" is not a different world with a different stock — it is the same paths with different weights, same volatility, which is what Girsanov means and what the experiment shows bin for bin (§5).

What it is trying to achieve for the course: it closes the loop on the thesis. The forward's price is pure no-arbitrage, no model needed; the page shows the same expectation reproduces it exactly (§3), so the risk-neutral average is no-arbitrage written as an average, and for the option — where no-arbitrage alone does not give a number — the average is what fills the gap. It also sets up 2.9 and 3.3: Feynman–Kac says the average and the PDE are two descriptions of one function (§6), so the Monte Carlo pricer, the closed form and the grid solver must agree, and 2.9 is just this page's expectation done by hand.

What it is not: a claim that the stock drifts at r, or that anyone is neutral to risk. Nobody is; the hedge is.

The so what

2Three questions a developer writing a Monte Carlo pricer asks

2.7 ended with μ gone from the PDE and a question: if the price does not depend on the stock's real-world drift, what drift goes into the simulator? A developer who writes "simulate the stock, average the discounted payoff" has to type a number for μ, and the obvious one — the stock's expected return — gives the wrong price. The page is the three decisions that follow from that, each with a wrong answer that produces a plausible-looking number.

"Rung" below means a step of the argument, laid out as a six-rung ladder in the bridge after §5 (theory after numbers). In one line each: 1 2.7's result — the hedged book earns r whatever μ is, and the PDE has no μ in it; 2 so price with the drift that makes the arithmetic easiest, r, "as if" the stock drifted at r; 3 under that drift the discounted stock is 2.5's exponential martingale; 4 the price as an expectation, V = e^(−rT)·E^Q[payoff], and Feynman–Kac: that expectation solves the PDE; 5 Girsanov — the change of drift is a reweighting of the same paths by L; 6 why "risk-neutral": under Q every discounted traded price is a martingale. Every "rung n" in the notes is a link to it.

1 · Which drift do you simulate under?
r. Not the stock's expected return, not zero, not "it doesn't matter". At μ = 0.1 the discounted real-world average is 21.42, 3.40 above the price; at μ = 0.3 it is 38.96; at μ = 0 it is 15.11, below. At μ = r it is 18.01 ± 0.03. The forward behaves the same way: only the r-drift returns S₀ − Ke^(−rT), the value that needed no simulation at all. The wrong answers are "the price is the expected payoff, discounted" and "μ doesn't matter, so any drift will do". §3.
2 · How many paths — and how do you know the number is right?
You don't get the price; you get the price ± a standard error, and the s.e. is sd/√n: 10³ paths give ±0.95 on 18, 10⁶ give ±0.03, 10⁸ would give ±0.003. The s.e. is not a guess: cut the run into batches and the batch means scatter by exactly it (0.298 measured against 0.312 reported at n = 10⁴). The wrong answers are "the Monte Carlo price is the price" and "more paths fix it linearly". §4.
3 · Can you reuse paths drawn at another drift?
Yes — weight each path by L = e^(−λW_T − ½λ²T), λ = (μ − r)/σ, and the μ-paths average to the price: 18.00 ± 0.01 from paths drawn at μ = 0.3, 18.11 ± 0.07 from μ = −0.2. Nothing about the paths changed: same σ (0.3994 against 0.3999), same W. What changed is the weight, and the weights move the mean drift from μ to r exactly. That is Girsanov, and its cost is paths: the effective sample size is n·e^(−λ²T), 68 % at |λ| = 0.625, 16 % at 1.375. The wrong answers are "changing the drift changes the volatility" and "you have to re-simulate". §5.

The maths the page needs is six rungs long (the bridge, after §5), and 2.5 and 2.7 did four of them; what is new is "pick the drift" (rung 2) and "a martingale has no drift, and Itô's lemma says what that costs V" (rung 4). What is new in the numbers is that the whole thing is one identity read three ways: e^(−rT)·E^Q[payoff] is the price (question 1), its Monte Carlo estimate is that price ± sd/√n (question 2), and E^Q[·] = E^P[L·(·)] on the same paths (question 3).

Q1

You price a European call by Monte Carlo: draw S_T many times, average the discounted payoff e^(−rT)·max(S_T − K, 0). The drift you simulate with is:

  • r, the risk-free rate — and the average is the price within its standard error — 18.010 ± 0.031 against the closed form's 18.023 at 10⁶ paths. The stock does not drift at r; pricing as if it did gives the number 2.7's hedge breaks even at.
  • μ, the stock's expected return: the price is the expected payoff, discounted — the real-world expectation mistaken for the price.   • Any drift — the price does not depend on μ, so it does not matter — the price's independence of μ read as the expectation's. V does not depend on μ; e^(−rT)·E[payoff] does, from 6.77 at μ = −0.2 to 38.96 at 0.3. Only the r-drift makes the two coincide.
  • Zero drift: a fair game has no drift — "fair" measured in the wrong unit. With μ = 0 the average is 15.11, 2.9 below the price. The fair game is the discounted stock, and that needs the stock to drift at r, not at 0.
Q2

Why does a drift the stock does not have give the right price?

  • Because 2.7's hedged book earns r whatever μ is, so V cannot depend on μ, and r is the drift for which "discount the expectation" reproduces that V — under the r-drift the discounted stock is a martingale (mean 100.0 ± 0.1 at every checkpoint) and the discounted hedged book is too (2.7: mean 0 at every drift). The forward shows it with no model: e^(−rT)·E[S_T − K] is S₀ − Ke^(−rT) = 4.88 only at μ = r.
  • Because the stock really drifts at r on average — the device taken literally. Nothing says so: E[S_T] is S₀e^(μT) = 110.5 at μ = 0.1, and the page measures it. Q is a change of weights on the paths, not a claim about the stock.
  • Because investors are risk-neutral, so they discount the expected payoff at r — the name taken for the reason. No investor's attitude enters: the hedge removes μ, and the hedge is the same for everyone. Two desks that believe μ = 0.3 and μ = −0.2 both get 18.0 from their own paths once the weights are on.
  • It is an approximation that is good when μ is close to r — exactness mistaken for luck. The reweighted price is 18.05 ± 0.01 from paths drawn at μ = 0.6, twelve times r. The identity E^Q[X] = E^P[L·X] holds at every λ; what grows with λ is the cost in effective paths, n·e^(−λ²T).
Q3

Under the r-drift, the discounted stock price e^(−rt)S_t is:

  • A martingale: its mean is S₀ at every t, with a spread that grows with t — 99.98, 100.00, 99.99, 100.01 (± 0.1) at t = ¼, ½, ¾, 1, sd 20 → 42. Path by path it is S₀·e^(σW̃_t − ½σ²t), 2.5's exponential martingale.
  • A constant: discounting at r undoes the growth at r — the mean mistaken for the path. It undoes the growth of the mean; each path still has σ dW̃ in it. The sd at T is 41.8 on a mean of 100.
  • A submartingale, because stocks go up on average — the real-world drift smuggled into Q. Under μ = 0.3 it is: 128.6 ± 0.2 at T. Under the r-drift the growth at r is exactly what the discounting removes, and the mean stays at 100.
  • A martingale under the real-world drift too, since martingale is a property of the Brownian motion, not the drift — drift and noise confused. The noise is the same under both; the drift is not, and the drift is what a martingale forbids. At μ = −0.2 the mean falls to 77.8 by T, at μ = 0.3 it rises to 128.6.
Q4

You have 10⁶ paths simulated at the stock's real-world drift μ = 0.3. To price under Q you:

  • Keep the paths and weight each by L = e^(−λW_T − ½λ²T), λ = (μ − r)/σ; the weighted average is the price, the paths' volatility is unchanged, and the weights cost effective paths, n·e^(−λ²T) — 18.00 ± 0.01 from the μ = 0.3 paths; realised vol 0.3994 weighted against 0.3999 as drawn; mean log-return moved from 0.222 to −0.029; ESS 68 % of n at λ = 0.625.
  • Re-simulate: a different drift means different paths — the change of measure mistaken for a change of model. A μ-path is a Q-path with a different probability, not a different shape: S_T = S₀·e^((r − ½σ²)T + σ(W_T + λT)) is the same number. Girsanov is the weight, and the histogram of the weighted μ-paths lies on the Q-run's bin for bin.
  • Rescale the volatility to σ − λ and keep the drift — drift and volatility traded off. Q changes the drift only. The weighted paths' realised vol is 0.3994 against σ = 0.4; a model with σ = 0.4 − 0.625 would not price anything.
  • Divide the average by e^((μ−r)T) — the stock's growth factor applied to the option. That fixes E[S_T] (134.9 → 105.1) and not the payoff: 38.96 / e^(0.25) = 30.3, not 18.0. The correction is per path, not per average, because the payoff is not linear in S_T. (For the forward it would work: linear.)
Q5

Your pricer reports 18.35 ± 0.31 from 10⁴ paths; the closed form is 18.02. The pricer is:

  • Right: the error is 1.1 standard errors, and the s.e. it reports is the error it makes — 100 independent runs of 10⁴ paths scatter by 0.298 against a reported 0.312 — and the error falls like 1/√n: ±0.95, ±0.31, ±0.10, ±0.03 at 10³ … 10⁶ paths.   • Wrong: it should return 18.02 — the estimate mistaken for the quantity. A Monte Carlo price is a price ± an s.e.; without the s.e. it is not a number. 18.35 ± 0.31 and 18.02 agree.
  • Right, and 10× the paths will give 10× the precision — 1/n assumed. It is 1/√n: 10× the paths gives √10 ≈ 3.2× the precision; four decimals need 10⁸ paths, not 10⁵. Slope −0.51 measured.
