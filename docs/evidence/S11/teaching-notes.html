What this page is about, and what it is trying to achieve

Pages 2.6 to 2.10 built the Black–Scholes model on three assumptions and used each of them: one known volatility σ for the life of the option; rebalancing that is continuous and costs nothing; and lognormal returns. This page breaks the three in turn, on a market built so that every price is still a closed form, and measures what breaks with them. Then it shows why the formula survives anyway: not as a model of the stock but as the way prices are quoted. "40 vol" is a price in different units, and the units are the point.

What the page achieves, in order: (1) one price has exactly one implied vol, because vega is positive everywhere, and the solver that finds it round-trips every row of the golden file; (2) a market where the stock's vol is 0.2 or 0.6 with equal chance has Black–Scholes implied vols that are not constant: a smile, lowest at the forward (0.398), rising to 0.48 at K = 60, and below the rms vol 0.447 at the money; (3) the mixture's returns have excess kurtosis 1.8 against the lognormal's 0, and 2.6's test battery catches it at the fourth moment, not the second; (4) hedged with Black–Scholes deltas at the implied vol, the book's spread stops falling at 8.1 while 2.7's falls to 0.21, and 7.54 of the 8.1 is uncertainty about which vol state the option was sold in, fixed before the state became known: incompleteness, not a hedge failing; (5) a 10 bp fee makes the rebalancing cost grow like √N, doubling for every fourfold N, so the trade-off has an interior optimum; (6) when the stock moves from 100 to 110 the price quote 18.02 is stale and the vol quote 0.40 is not.

Why a developer cares: every pricer in production takes a vol surface in, not a σ, and every risk system reports against it. The implied-vol solver is the first root finder in the course and the piece of Part 3 (3.4) that every desk tool calls most. Knowing which assumption breaks which number is what separates a pricer that is wrong from one that is wrong in a known direction.

The floor, and what it is made of. The control's spread falls as 1/√N, as on 2.7. The Black–Scholes hedge on the mixture stops at 8.08 and is still at 8.08 at N = 4096, forty times the control. Split the paths by their state: on the low-vol paths the mean P&L is −7.530 ± 0.042 and on the high-vol paths +7.624 ± 0.080, against the two states' price gaps C(0.2) − 17.987 = −7.536 and C(0.6) − 17.987 = +7.536, at every N (within 5 s.e. at all six). Those two means are ±7.54 apart, and a coin toss between them has sd 7.536. That is the between-state spread: uncertainty about which state the option was sold in, settled before the state became known. The option was bought at a price that was right on average and wrong in both states; with only the stock to trade, the state is a risk nothing in the market spans. It is incompleteness, not a hedge failing. The remaining within-state spread is 2.83 for the Black–Scholes hedge (1.90 on the low paths, 3.53 on the high), and it too has stopped falling: it is 2.7's gamma P&L with a wrong σi, (σj² − σi²) × the gamma integral, a path integral that does not shrink with the step. Overall 8.08² ≈ 7.54² + 2.83².

The oracle hedge separates the two. Knowing the path's vol and hedging at it, the within-state spread falls like 1/√N (0.927, 0.467, 0.230 at N = 256, 1024, 4096: slope −0.4995, 2.7's replication working perfectly in each state), and the overall spread converges to 7.542, within 0.1 % of the between-state 7.536. The right Greeks remove the hedging error; the between-state piece is not a hedging error, so no Greeks touch it. The mean P&L is zero in all three books at every N (−0.16 ± 0.13 for the Black–Scholes hedge at N = 4096), which is 2.8's statement that the price is the expectation under the pricing drift whatever you do afterwards.

2.7 saw this floor one vol at a time: its off-vol scenarios (σr = 0.3 and 0.5 at σi = 0.4) had spreads that stopped at about 1.2 and 1.6. Here the two states sit inside one book, so the floor has the price gap in it as well.

F · Vol is the unit of price

The desk quotes the one-year 100 call at "40 vol" rather than "18.02". The stock moves from 100 to 110. At 40 vol the call is now worth 24.74, so the price quote is 6.72 stale; read the stale 18.02 at the new spot as a vol and it says 0.211, which nobody believes. The vol quote has not moved: if the market still thinks 40 vol, 40 is still right, and the price follows from it with the new inputs. Quoting in vol removes the inputs that are usually observable (S, K, r, T) and leaves volatility, the quantity options are primarily quoted and compared in (your wording, 2026-11-09). It makes a 60-strike and a 160-strike comparable (0.477 against 0.452: the market pays more, in vol terms, for the low strike), and it is why the market's real object is the surface of implied vols, which 3.4 builds and D9 draws.

Why — the reasoning, after the numbers

1

One price, one vol. ∂C/∂σ = vega = S₀φ(d₁)√T, and φ is positive everywhere, so C is strictly increasing in σ. As σ → 0, d₁ and d₂ run to ±∞ together with the sign of ln(S₀/K) + rT and C → max(S₀ − Ke−rT, 0); as σ → ∞, d₁ → +∞ and d₂ → −∞ and C → S₀. A continuous, strictly increasing function hits each value between its limits once. Newton converges quadratically because vega is smooth and does not vanish near the root; the bisection guard is for the wings, where vega is tiny and a Newton step can overshoot the bracket.

2

Why the mixture smiles, and why its bottom is at the forward. Far from the money the low-vol component is worth almost nothing, so the mixture's price is close to half the high-vol component's and its implied vol climbs towards 0.6. At the forward, C(F) = e−rTF(2N(σ√T/2) − 1) is almost linear in σ and slightly concave, so the average of the two prices sits just under the price at the average vol, 0.398 against 0.40; it is concave in σ² (a square root), so it sits well under the price at the rms vol. The symmetry σ(K) = σ(F²/K) is the lognormal's reflection symmetry about its forward, which each component has and the mixture, being a weighted sum of components with the same forward, inherits. Break the equal forwards (the skewed mixture) and the symmetry goes with them: that is skew.

3

Why 2.6's battery catches it at the fourth moment. A mixture of two normals with the same mean has the same first two moments as a normal with the pooled variance: by construction. Its fourth moment is 3E[σ⁴] against the normal's 3E[σ²]², and E[σ⁴] > E[σ²]² whenever σ varies (Jensen again): excess kurtosis 3E[σ⁴]/E[σ²]² − 3 = 1.92 for daily returns, 1.80 at the one-year horizon where the two means also differ. That is why "mean and variance match" says nothing about the tails, and why the smile exists: option prices are integrals against the tails.

4

Why the floor, and why it splits. Under the pricing drift the discounted stock is a martingale, so every trading strategy's discounted gains have mean zero and E[P&L | state j] = C(σj) − P₀ for any hedge and any N. The law of total variance then reads Var(P&L) = Var(E[P&L | j]) + E[Var(P&L | j)]: the first term is √(p(1 − p))·|C(0.6) − C(0.2)| = 7.54, the unspanned state — incompleteness, which no strategy in the stock alone touches; the second is the replication error within a state. For the oracle hedge the second term is 2.7's 1/√N error and vanishes. For the Black–Scholes hedge at σi it is 2.7's gamma P&L, (σj² − σi²)∫e−rt½ΓiS²dt, a path integral whose spread does not fall with N: 2.83 here.

5

Why √N. One rebalance trades |Δi+1 − Δi| ≈ Γ S |δS| shares, and |δS| ≈ Sσ√δt·|Z| has mean Sσ√(T/N)·√(2/π). N of them: cost ≈ k·Γ·S²·σ·√(2T/π)·√N on average, with Γ and S along the path. The ratio for N → 4N is 2 exactly in the limit; the measured 2.42 at the first step is the setup and unwind not yet negligible against four trades.

Decisions you may overrule

  • D1 — drift μ = r on every path set, not 2.7's μ = 0. Under the pricing drift the mean hedged P&L is zero for every N and every hedge (2.8), so the per-state means are the price gaps exactly and the spread carries the whole story. 2.7 showed the hedge is drift-blind in the limit, so the choice costs nothing; its sd·√N = 13.54 at μ = 0 and the control's 13.21 at μ = r agree within the seeds' noise. Alternative: μ = 0 as on 2.7, at the cost of a small drift leakage in the per-state means at low N.
  • D2 — the skewed mixture's parameters: p = ¾ / ¼, σ = 0.3 / 0.6, forwards 1.05 F / 0.85 F. Chosen so the forward is preserved and the tilt is visible (0.08 across the strikes) without a wing going beyond the solver's cap. Any other asymmetric pair tells the same story.
  • D3 — k = 10 basis points per unit of stock traded, charged at setup, each rebalance and the unwind; the setup and unwind reported separately so the √N law is seen on the rebalancing alone.
  • D4 — the golden file's three vega classes (≥ 1; 10⁻⁸ to 1; < 10⁻⁸) with the check on the middle class being the error bound price_tol × max(1, price) / vega. The handover asked for 10⁻¹⁰ "where vega is not vanishing"; vega ≥ 1 is where that holds for a 10⁻¹² price, and the bound is the honest statement in between.
  • D5 — "spread + cost" adds an sd to a mean; it is a reading aid, not a risk measure. The net P&L's sd is also tabled, and its minimum (N = 1024) differs from the sum's (N = 256). Leland's criterion is v2's.
  • D6 — 2.6's battery against the lognormal fitted to the first two log moments (σfit = 0.454), not against σ = 0.4 or the rms 0.447. Fitting the two moments is what makes "which check catches it" a fair question.
  • D7 — the oracle hedge is on the page as the device that separates the two pieces of the floor. It is not a strategy anyone can run; it is the measurement that says what the Black–Scholes hedge is paying for.

Checks Q1–Q6 (I5) — approved 2026-11-09

One per section A–F. Every wrong option names the misconception it encodes; the feedback says why it is wrong. Your P1–P5 traps are the distractors marked ⚑.

Q1 · A market quotes the one-year 100-strike call at 18.02 (S₀ = 100, r = 0.05). How many values of σ make the Black–Scholes formula return that price?
  1. ✓ Exactly one — vega = S₀φ(d₁)√T is positive at every σ, so the price is strictly increasing in σ and crosses 18.02 once. The solver finds 0.4000 in four Newton steps.
  2. ✗ ⚑ Two — the price curve in σ is a bowl and a horizontal line cuts it twice. — vega's sign misread. The price is monotone in σ; it is vega that has a hump, not the price.
  3. ✗ One for any positive price. — the bounds forgotten. Only prices between S₀ − Ke^(−rT) = 4.88 (σ → 0) and S₀ = 100 (σ → ∞) have a vol; the solver refuses 4.0 and 101.
  4. ✗ It depends on the starting guess — Newton can land on different roots. — the root finder blamed for the function. There is one root; a bad guess slows Newton down or needs a bisection step, it does not change the answer.
Q2 · With probability ½ the stock's vol is 0.2, with probability ½ it is 0.6, same forward. You back out Black–Scholes implied vols from the mixture's prices at every strike. The curve against strike is:
  1. ✓ A smile, lowest at the forward (0.398) and rising in both wings — and the at-the-money vol is below the rms vol 0.447. 0.477 at K = 60, 0.452 at K = 160; the fat tails make the wings dear.
  2. ✗ ⚑ Flat at 0.447 — the mixture has the same variance as a lognormal at the rms vol. — variance averaged, prices not. Prices are concave in σ² near the money and the wings weigh the tails; the same second moment does not give the same prices.
  3. ✗ Flat at 0.40 — the market is just the average-vol model. — the mixture mistaken for its mean. At the money the price is nearly the price at 0.40 (0.399), but at K = 60 the implied vol is 0.477: the mixture is not any single lognormal.
  4. ✗ A skew sloping down to the right — high vol goes with low prices. — asymmetry assumed. With equal forwards the smile is symmetric in log-moneyness to 10⁻¹⁵; skew needs the high-vol state to carry a lower forward.
Q3 · The mixture passes 2.6's checks on the mean and variance of log ST against a lognormal fitted to them. Which of 2.6's checks catches it?
  1. ✓ The excess-kurtosis check — 1.79 against 0, 365 standard errors away (and the skewness and median checks fail with it). Each vol state alone is Gaussian; the fat tails are the mixing.
  2. ✗ None — two models with the same mean and variance price every option the same. — two moments mistaken for the whole distribution. Option prices are integrals against the tails; the smile is the difference made visible.
  3. ✗ E[ST] = S₀e^(rT). — the forward mistaken for a model property. Both models have forward F by no-arbitrage; the check passes (z = −0.9) and says nothing about which model is right.
  4. ✗ The sd of ST to 3 %. — price-space variance too blunt. The mixture's sd of S_T is 1.4 % above the fitted lognormal's and passes 2.6's 3 % rule.
Q4 · You hedge the mixture market with Black–Scholes deltas at the implied vol 0.399, rebalancing N times. As N → ∞ the hedged P&L's spread:
  1. ✓ Stops at about 8.1 — 7.5 of it is uncertainty about which vol state the option was sold in, fixed before the state became known, and 2.8 is the Greeks being wrong for the path. 8.136, 8.091, 8.080 at N = 256, 1024, 4096.
  2. ✗ ⚑ Falls like 1/√N to zero, as on 2.7. — discreteness taken for the only error. 2.7's rate needs the model's σ to be the path's σ; here it is neither 0.2 nor 0.6.
  3. ✗ Falls to zero, only more slowly. — a floor misread as a slower rate. The log-log slope over N ≥ 64 is −0.004: the spread is not falling at all.
  4. ✗ Stops at a floor that knowing each path's true vol would remove entirely. — incompleteness confused with hedging error. The oracle hedge brings the within-state spread to 0.23 and the overall spread to 7.54: the option was bought before the state was known, and the stock alone cannot span the state.
Q5 · A fee of k on every share traded. You double the number of rebalances N. The total rebalancing cost is multiplied by about:
  1. ✓ √2 ≈ 1.41 — each trade is ∝ 1/√N smaller and there are twice as many. Measured: a fourfold N doubles the cost (ratio 2.000 from N = 256 on), so a doubling multiplies it by √2.
  2. ✗ ⚑ 2 — twice as many trades. — the trades assumed the same size. A rebalance trades |δΔ| ≈ ΓS|δS| and |δS| shrinks like √δt.
  3. ✗ 1 — the trades shrink as fast as they multiply. — 1/√N mistaken for 1/N. The size falls like 1/√N, the count rises like N; N × 1/√N = √N grows.
  4. ✗ ⚑ It does not matter — in the limit the spread goes to zero and that is what counts. — the limit taken without the fee. At N = 4096 the fee is 2.07 against a spread of 0.21; spread + cost is smallest at N = 256.
Q6 · The desk quotes the one-year 100 call at "40 vol" rather than "18.02". The stock moves from 100 to 110. Which quote has gone stale?
  1. ✓ The price — at 40 vol the call is now worth 24.74; the vol quote still stands if the market still thinks 40 vol. Vol removes the inputs that are usually observable, S, K, r and T, and leaves volatility, the quantity options are quoted and compared in.
  2. ✗ ⚑ The vol — 40 % was right when the stock was at 100. — the vol treated as a function of the spot. The implied vol is the market's view of σ; the spot is an input, not a reason for it to change. Re-read at 110, the stale 18.02 would mean 0.211.
  3. ✗ Both — every input moved, so every quote is stale. — the two quotes treated as the same thing. One is a price; the other is a price with the known inputs divided out.
  4. ✗ Neither — a quote is a quote until it is withdrawn. — the market's convention ignored. A price quote without its spot is meaningless a minute later; that is why desks quote in vol.
