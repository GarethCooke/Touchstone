Step 3 · predictions 1 and 3 resolved

3Wild terms, tame sum

You said Σ(ΔW)² settles on 1 and that two paths give the same value. Both right. The interesting part is that neither is obvious from the terms. Work in units of dt throughout: ΔW = √dt·Z with Z ~ N(0, 1), so one term is (ΔW)² = dt·Z². Z² has mean 1 and variance E[Z⁴] − (E[Z²])² = 3 − 1 = 2 — the 3 is the Gaussian fourth moment, and it is where the 2 comes from. So one term has mean dt and sd √2·dt — an sd bigger than the mean, on a quantity that lives on [0, ∞). That already says the term is lopsided (a square cannot go negative, so the spread has to be mostly on the right), and it is: half of all terms are below 0.45·dt (the median of Z²), a quarter below 0.10·dt, one in twenty above 3.84·dt, one in 370 above 9·dt (|Z| > 3). The mean of dt sits well to the right of the typical term, and "mean ± sd" is not a range here: the sd is the root-mean-square distance from the mean, and for Z² it is made of many small shortfalls on the left and a few big overshoots on the right.

What squaring does to a Gaussian

If X ~ N(0, σ²), then X² has mean σ² and variance 2σ⁴ — the variance of the square is twice the square of the variance. The 2 comes from the fourth moment: E[X⁴] = 3σ⁴ for any Gaussian (the kurtosis 3 from 2.2's C1), so Var(X²) = E[X⁴] − (E[X²])² = 3σ⁴ − σ⁴ = 2σ⁴. Here σ² = dt, giving mean dt and variance 2dt². The shape changes too: X was symmetric about 0; X² lives on [0, ∞), piles up against 0 (its density blows up like 1/√x there) and trails off to the right — the χ² distribution with one degree of freedom. That is why the median 0.45·dt sits well below the mean dt, and why "mean ± sd" is the wrong picture for one term.

Now sum n of them, still in units of dt:

  • Σ(ΔW)²/dt = Σ Zᵢ² — n independent Z² terms.
  • Mean of the sum: n × 1 = n.
  • Variance of the sum: n × 2 = 2n, so sd √(2n).
  • Multiply back by dt = T/n. Scaling a variable by dt scales its mean and sd by dt and its variance by dt²: mean n·dt = T; variance 2n·dt² = 2T²/n; sd √(2n)·dt = T·√(2/n).
  • Relative spread, sd ÷ mean: √2 for one term → √(2/n) for the sum.

In numbers, T = 1:

  • Mean of the sum: 1, at every n.
  • Spread of the sum: 0.14 at n = 100 · 0.014 at n = 10,000 · 0.0014 at n = 1,000,000. Ten times more steps, √10 times less spread.
  • Fine enough grid, and every path gives 1. That is all "path by path" means.

Honest footnote

The last column is a different question from the one before it: not "where did the path end" but "how far did it wander". Correlation of Σ(ΔW)² with max|W| is not zero: 0.023 in the 20,000-path run (the 400-path values above it are noise around something small and positive). Bigger kicks make bigger excursions, so a path whose Σ(ΔW)² came out high tends to have wandered further. The effect is of order 1/√n and the check bounds it at 0.05 rather than asserting 0. W_T is different — its correlation with Σ(ΔW)² is exactly zero. Why: W_T = Σ ΔW, so the covariance of Σ(ΔW)² with W_T is a double sum of Cov((ΔWᵢ)², ΔWⱼ). Different steps are independent, so every i ≠ j term is 0. The i = j terms are Cov((ΔW)², ΔW) = E[(ΔW)³] − E[(ΔW)²]·E[ΔW] = E[(ΔW)³] − 0. And E[(ΔW)³] = 0 because ΔW is symmetric about 0: every positive value of (ΔW)³ has an equally likely negative twin, and they cancel. ("Odd moment" means E[X^k] with k odd — 1, 3, 5 — and for any distribution symmetric about 0 they are all zero.) Zero covariance, so zero correlation, exactly and at every n; the −0.007 is sampling noise around it.

Step 4 · prediction 2 resolved

4The path has no length

You said Σ|ΔW| settles on 1. It does not settle at all. The same one-step calculation: |ΔW| is √dt · |Z|, and E|Z| = √(2/π) = 0.798, so E|ΔW| = 0.798·√dt. Sum n of them: 0.798 · n · √dt = 0.798 · n · √(T/n) = 0.798 · √(nT) — √n when T = 1, as here. Ten times more steps, √10 = 3.16 times more distance. The path's length grows without limit as you measure it more finely — a path drawn on paper has a length; W does not.

Where √(2/π) comes from

Two pieces. The bell curve itself is φ(z) = e^(−z²/2)/√(2π) — the density of a standard normal, the height of the bell at z; the √(2π) is just the constant that makes the total area 1. An expectation is the probability-weighted average from the S1 ledger: for any function g, E[g(Z)] = ∫ g(z)·φ(z) dz — value times how likely, summed over all z. So E|Z| is g(z) = |z| under that formula: ∫ |z| · e^(−z²/2)/√(2π) dz. (Same recipe gives E[Z²] = ∫ z²·φ(z) dz = 1 and E[Z⁴] = ∫ z⁴·φ(z) dz = 3, the two numbers §3 leaned on.) The bell is symmetric, so fold the negative half onto the positive half and double: 2/√(2π) · ∫₀^∞ z·e^(−z²/2) dz. That last integral is the one Gaussian integral that is easy — the z in front is the derivative of −z²/2, so the antiderivative is −e^(−z²/2), which runs from −1 at z = 0 to 0 at z = ∞: the integral is 1. Hence E|Z| = 2/√(2π) = √(2/π) = 0.7979. Compare E[Z²] = 1: the average size of a standard normal draw is 0.80, less than its sd of 1, because the sd is a root-mean-square and squaring favours the big draws. Checked in the table: Σ|ΔW|/√(nT) lands on 0.798 at every n.

Log-log slope of Σ|ΔW| against n: +0.4999. The constant √(2/π) = 0.7979 is recovered to three decimals at every n. The coin walk from 2.1 — steps of exactly ±√dt — has Σ|ΔW| = n·√dt = n·√(T/n) = √(nT) with constant 1 and no randomness at all; the Gaussian's 0.798 is just E|Z|. Same story as 2.2's slope: the increments shrink like √dt, but there are 1/dt of them, and √dt · (1/dt) = 1/√dt → ∞.

Why P2 is the natural mistake: every curve you have ever drawn has finite length, and Σ|Δf| for a curve is its length. Your intuition was "a path is a curve, so it has a length". 2.2 already said W is not that kind of curve — no tangent anywhere — and this is the same fact seen from the other side.

Step 5 · prediction 4 resolved

5The smooth curve is the mirror image

P4 read Σ(Δf)² as a curve — "looks more like sin²(2πt)". It is a number: one sum over the whole grid, and the question is what number it tends to as n grows. For a smooth f, Δf ≈ f′(t)·dt, so (Δf)² ≈ f′(t)²·dt², and the sum of n of those is dt · Σ f′² dt ≈ dt · ∫f′². For f = sin(2πt), ∫₀¹ f′² = (2π)² · ½ = 2π² = 19.74, so Σ(Δf)² ≈ 19.74 / n → 0. And Σ|Δf| ≈ Σ|f′|·dt → ∫|f′| = 4, the total rise and fall of a sine wave over one period (up 1, down 2, up 1). Both measured exactly:

The calculus used here, spelled out

f(t) = sin(2πt) on [0, 1] is one full period of a sine wave. Four facts, then the two sums.

  • Chain rule. For f(t) = g(h(t)), f′(t) = g′(h(t)) · h′(t): differentiate the outside with the inside left alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside.
  • Derivative of f. Outside sin → cos; inside 2πt → 2π. So f′(t) = 2π·cos(2πt). The 2π is the factor the chain rule brings down.
  • ∫₀¹ f′(t)² dt = 2π². The sum Σ(Δf)² needs f′ squared. Square the derivative: f′² = (2π)²·cos²(2πt) = 4π²·cos²(2πt). A definite integral over [0, 1] is just the average value of the integrand, since the interval has length 1. So we need the average of cos² over one full period: the identity cos²θ = ½ + ½·cos(2θ), and cos(2θ) averages 0 over a period (as much above the axis as below), leaves ½. Integral: 4π² × ½ = 2π² = 19.74.
  • ∫₀¹ |f′(t)| dt = 4. The integral of |f′| is the total distance f travels up and down: 0 → 1 → −1 → 0 is 1 + 2 + 1 = 4. No integration needed.
  • Σ(Δf)²: each Δf ≈ f′·dt, so (Δf)² ≈ f′²·dt². Sum: Σ f′²·dt² = dt · Σ f′²·dt ≈ dt · ∫f′² = 2π²·dt = 19.74/n → 0.
  • Σ|Δf|: each |Δf| ≈ |f′|·dt. Sum: Σ |f′|·dt ≈ ∫|f′| = 4. Finite.

The two words in the title

"Variation" is how much a path moves, measured by adding up its steps. Add up |Δ·| and you have the total variation — for a curve, its length. Add up (Δ·)² and you have the quadratic variation ("quadratic" = squared), written [W]_T or ⟨W⟩_T and defined as the limit of Σ(ΔW)² as the grid is refined. A smooth curve has total variation and no quadratic variation (the squares vanish). W has quadratic variation T and no total variation (the absolute values blow up). The page's title is the name of the number Σ(ΔW)² settles on.

Step 6 · prediction 5 resolved

6Everything beyond order dt vanishes

P5 sent both cross terms to 1. The instinct is "Σ Δt = T, so a sum with a Δt in it lands on T". Count powers of dt instead. Every sum has n = T/dt terms; what matters is the size of one term.

  • Σ Δt — one term is dt. n × dt = T. Survives.
  • Σ(ΔW)² — one term is dt-sized (a √dt kick, squared). n × dt = T. Survives.
  • Σ ΔW·Δt — every Δt is the same number, dt, so it comes out of the sum: Σ ΔW·dt = dt · Σ ΔW. And Σ ΔW is just the path's total change, W_T (all the steps added up). So the sum is exactly dt · W_T — the endpoint, scaled down by dt. W_T is a fixed-size random number (sd √T, about ±1), and dt = T/n → 0, so dt · W_T → 0 like 1/n. Dies. (Path 1 in §2 shows it: −6.73 × 10⁻³, −6.73 × 10⁻⁴, … — the same W_T = −0.673 every time, divided by n.)
  • Σ(Δt)² — one term is dt². n × dt² = T·dt → 0 like 1/n. Dies.
  • Σ|ΔW|³ — one term is dt^{3/2}-sized. n × dt^{3/2} = T·√dt → 0 like 1/√n. Dies.

Rule: a term of size dt survives summation (n of them make T); anything smaller than dt does not.

Step 7 · the reason

7Why ordinary calculus fails here — and only here

Start with the simplest smooth function there is, g(x) = x², where the algebra is exact and there is nothing to approximate.

  • One step. (W + ΔW)² − W² = 2W·ΔW + (ΔW)². Two terms: one with ΔW, one with (ΔW)².
  • Sum over the grid. The left side telescopes to W_T² − 0. The right side is 2·Σ W·ΔW + Σ(ΔW)². So W_T² = 2·Σ W·ΔW + Σ(ΔW)².
  • Smooth path x(t) in place of W. Same identity: x_T² = 2·Σ x·Δx + Σ(Δx)². But Σ(Δx)² → 0 (§5), so x_T² = 2·∫x dx. That is the chain rule, d(x²) = 2x·dx, with nothing left over.
  • Brownian W. Σ(ΔW)² → T, not 0. So W_T² = 2·Σ W·ΔW + T. The chain rule's answer, 2·Σ W·ΔW, is short by exactly T. (Rearranged, Σ W·ΔW = ½W_T² − ½T — that is page 2.4's result, ∫W dW; not for 2.3 to claim, but this is where it comes from.)

Now the general case. For any smooth g, Taylor's theorem says the change over one small step is a series in powers of the step:

  • One step. g(W + ΔW) − g(W) = g′(W)·ΔW + ½g″(W)·(ΔW)² + ⅙g‴(W)·(ΔW)³ + … (g′, g″, g‴: first, second, third derivatives of g, all evaluated at the current W).
  • Term sizes. ΔW is √dt-sized, so the terms are √dt, dt, dt^{3/2}, … in size. Same power-counting as §6: after summing n of them, size dt survives and anything smaller dies.
  • Smooth path: Δx is dt-sized, so the terms are dt, dt², dt³, …. Only the first survives the sum: Σ g′(x)·Δx → ∫g′(x) dx. Chain rule.
  • Brownian W: first term survives as before. Second term: Σ ½g″(W)·(ΔW)² — n terms of size dt — survives, and since (dW)² = dt it sums to ½∫g″(W) dt. Third term: n terms of size dt^{3/2}, Σ|ΔW|³ → 0 like 1/√n. Dies, as does everything after it.

So for W the change in g(W) has one extra term the chain rule does not have, ½∫g″(W) dt, and nothing beyond it. For g = x², g″ = 2 and the extra term is ½·2·T = T, matching the exact calculation above.

That is the sentence the index promises: the whole reason ordinary calculus fails on W is that (dW)² = dt, not 0. Not that W is random (a random smooth path would be fine), not that it has no derivative as such (the increments are all we ever use) — but that its squared increments add up to something. Page 2.4 does the sum for g(W) = W² and gets ∫W dW = ½W_T² − ½T; the −½T is this term. Not for 2.3 to claim.

Step 8 · the coin, again

8The coin walk has it exactly

Build the walk from 2.1 with steps of exactly ±√dt. Then (ΔW)² = dt for every step, and Σ(ΔW)² = n·dt = T with no randomness and no limit needed. The JSON checks this at every n, every path: the largest deviation from T is 4 × 10⁻¹⁶, floating-point rounding. The Gaussian walk earns by convergence what the coin has by construction; the T·√(2/n) spread in §3 is the price of Gaussian steps, and it goes to zero. Same limit, both coins — 2.1's C4 and 2.2's kurtosis story again, now for the quadratic variation.

Step 9 · for your approval

9Draft check questions

Per I5: every wrong option names the misconception it encodes, and feedback says why it is wrong. Q2, Q3's second option and Q4 are your own pre-run predictions. Say which to keep, cut or reword.

Q1

A Brownian path on [0, 1] is chopped into n equal steps and you compute Σ(ΔW)². As n → ∞ it:

  • Settles on 1, with spread √(2/n) — each (ΔW)² averages dt, and there are n = 1/dt of them.
  • Grows without limit — confused with total distance. That is Σ|ΔW|. Squaring a √dt-sized kick gives a dt-sized term, and n of those sum to T, not to ∞.
  • Shrinks to 0 — smooth-function intuition. For a smooth curve (Δf)² is dt²-sized and the sum dies like 1/n. W's increments are √dt-sized, not dt-sized; that one power is the whole difference.
  • Lands on a random number that depends on the path — quadratic variation as a path property. The spread across paths is √(2/n) and vanishes: at n = 10⁶, 400 paths all landed within 0.005 of 1.
Q2

Same path, same grids. Σ|ΔW| — the total distance travelled — as n → ∞:

  • Grows without limit, like √n — E|ΔW| = √(2dt/π), and n of those make 0.798·n·√dt = 0.798·√(nT).
  • Settles on the path's length — every drawn curve has a length. A curve with a tangent does; W has none (2.2). Its increments shrink like √dt while their number grows like 1/dt, and √dt/dt → ∞.
  • Settles on 1, like Σ(ΔW)² — squared and absolute variation confused. Squaring a √dt-sized term makes it dt-sized, small enough for n of them to sum to T. Not squaring leaves it √dt-sized, too big.
  • Shrinks to 0, since every |ΔW| → 0 — vanishing terms mean a vanishing sum. Each term is √dt-sized, but there are 1/dt of them. The count wins.
Q3

Replace W by the smooth curve f(t) = sin(2πt) on the same grids. As n → ∞, Σ(Δf)² and Σ|Δf|:

  • Σ(Δf)² → 0 like 1/n; Σ|Δf| → 4 — Δf ≈ f′dt, so squares are dt²-sized and vanish, while absolute values sum to ∫|f′|, the curve's total rise and fall.
  • Σ(Δf)² → ∫f² = ½ — increments confused with values. Σ(Δf)² adds up squared changes, not squared heights. Each change is dt-sized, its square dt²-sized, and n·dt² → 0.
  • Both → ∞, as for W — W's behaviour generalised to every path. f has a derivative, so Δf ≈ f′·dt is dt-sized, not √dt-sized. Everything W does traces back to that missing √.
  • Σ(Δf)² → a positive number and Σ|Δf| → ∞ — smooth and rough swapped. That is W's pattern exactly. A smooth curve has length and no quadratic variation; W has quadratic variation and no length.
Q4

On the same grids, Σ ΔW·Δt and Σ(Δt)² as n → ∞:

  • Both → 0 like 1/n — Σ ΔW·Δt = dt·W_T and Σ(Δt)² = T·dt; one extra factor of dt kills each.
  • Both → T — "Δt sums to T, so anything with a Δt in it does". Σ Δt = T because n terms of size dt make n·dt. Multiply each term by another dt, or by a √dt-sized ΔW, and the sum shrinks by that factor.
  • Σ ΔW·Δt → W_T and Σ(Δt)² → 0 — one factor forgotten. Σ ΔW = W_T, yes. But each term also carries a Δt = dt, so the sum is dt·W_T, which is W_T/n → 0.
  • Σ ΔW·Δt → 0 but Σ(Δt)² → T — squaring keeps dt alive, as it did for dW. (dW)² sums to T because ΔW is √dt-sized and squaring brings it to dt. Δt is already dt-sized; squaring takes it to dt², and n·dt² = T·dt → 0.
Q5

Expand g(W + ΔW) − g(W) by Taylor and sum over the grid. Which term behaves differently for W than for a smooth path, and why?

  • The second-order term ½g″(ΔW)², because Σ(ΔW)² → T instead of 0 — it survives, and the chain rule is off by ½∫g″ dt.
  • None — first order is enough, as in ordinary calculus — the chain rule assumed. First order is enough only when Σ(ΔW)² → 0. It is T. That is the one fact this page exists to establish.
  • Every term — W is too rough for Taylor to apply — rough means no expansion. Taylor expands g, which is smooth; W only supplies the increments. Third order and beyond still vanish: Σ|ΔW|³ → 0 like 1/√n.
  • The first-order term, since W has no derivative — no derivative, no first-order term. g′(W)·ΔW needs W's increment, not W's derivative. The increments are all stochastic calculus ever uses.

Alternative Q5, if you would rather keep the Taylor argument for 2.4: the coin walk (§8) — "with steps of exactly ±√dt, Σ(ΔW)² is: exactly T for every path ✓ / approximately T, converging ✗ (Gaussian intuition applied to the coin) / random ✗ / 0 ✗". Your call.
