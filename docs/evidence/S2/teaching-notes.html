Step 2 · the object

2W is 2.1's walk with a name, and four facts are its whole definition

Take the walk of 2.1 with Gaussian steps of size √dt and let dt → 0. What survives is one random function of time, written W (Wiener) or B (Brown). Everything anyone ever proves about it comes from four properties, and together they are the definition:

D1
Starts at zero. W₀ = 0.
D2
Independent increments. What W does over [s, t] is independent of everything it did before s.
D3
Gaussian increments with variance equal to elapsed time. W_t − W_s ~ N(0, t − s). Not sd = t − s; not sd = √t − √s. Variance is what adds (2.1), so variance is what is proportional to time.
D4
Continuous paths. No jumps. This is the one that is easy to say and expensive to prove; the experiment shows it rather than proves it.

Simulation is D2 and D3 read literally — draw independent N(0, dt) increments and add them up. This is the line the site's demos, the C++ and this experiment all share:

np.cumsum(sampler(rng, (k, n_steps)) * sqrt_dt, axis=1, out=w[:, 1:])   # W = Σ √dt·Z

Why Gaussian — the coin says so

D3 looks like an assumption. It isn't: it is what the continuous limit produces from any finite-variance step, by the central limit theorem. The experiment builds a second W from ±1 coin steps and measures the shape of its increments over k steps. A k-step coin increment is a scaled binomial with excess kurtosis exactly −2/k, and the measurement lands on that line:

steps in the increment, kexcess kurtosis, coin walk−2/kexcess kurtosis, Gaussian walk

One coin step is as un-Gaussian as a distribution gets (two points). Over 250 steps the coin walk and the Gaussian walk are indistinguishable at this sample size. The limit doesn't remember the coin — 2.1's C4, now for the shape as well as the scale.

Step 4 · prediction 2 resolved

4Continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere

You predicted the secant slope (W(t+h) − W(t))/h flattens toward 0 as h shrinks. Every smooth function does that — the zigzags average out and a tangent appears. W does the opposite, and by exactly the amount 2.1 predicts: the increment over h has sd √h, so the slope has sd √h / h = 1/√h. Halve h and the slope's typical size grows by √2. It never settles.

Consequence you will meet on every later page

dW/dt does not exist, so dW is never a derivative. It is the increment W_(t+dt) − W_t, a √dt-sized kick (ledger, S1 batch). Ordinary calculus is built on tangents; W has none. That is why 2.3 has to invent a replacement rule — (dW)² = dt — and why 2.4 has to redefine the integral. Nothing on this page claims either yet.

Step 6 · prediction 3 resolved

6Martingale and Markov: the best forecast is where you are, and the spread is the time still to come

W(1) = 1.5. You said E[W(2)] = 1.5 — right, and it has a name: W is a martingale. E[W_t | everything up to s] = W_s. No drift toward zero, no momentum; the past has already happened and the future increment has mean zero (D2, D3).

You said the spread is √2. That is the spread of W(2) seen from time 0 — Var W(2) = 2 — and the experiment measures it: 1.419. But you are standing at time 1 knowing W(1). The only randomness left is the increment over [1, 2], and its variance is 2 − 1 = 1. Conditioning removes the variance already realised. sd 1, not √2.

Two ways to measure "given W(1)" — filter, or fit a line

Your question was about paths that happen to be at 1.5 at time 1. The direct measurement is a filter: of 40,000 paths, keep the 969 whose W(1) lands in [1.4, 1.6], and look at where they are at time 2. Their mean W(2) is 1.487 against their mean W(1) of 1.497, and their sd is 0.971. Standard error on that mean is 1/√969 ≈ 0.03, so 1.487 and 1.497 are the same number. That is P3 answered literally — but it uses 969 paths out of 40,000 and only answers for 1.5.

Regression is the same question asked at every level at once. Plot 40,000 points, W(1) on the x-axis and W(2) on the y-axis, and fit the straight line W(2) ≈ slope · W(1) + intercept by least squares. The line is the forecast of W(2) from W(1). Its three numbers each say something specific. Slope 1: a path at W(1) = x is forecast to be at x — the whole of where you are carries forward, no more (momentum would give > 1), no less (mean reversion would give < 1). Intercept 0: the forecast has no fixed offset, so there is no level the path is pulled toward. Residual variance: the spread of the points around the line — the part of W(2) that W(1) cannot forecast. It comes out at 1.006, which is Var(W(2)) − Var(W(1)) = 2 − 1: exactly the variance of the increment over [1, 2], and nothing else.

The Markov test is the same fit with a second input. Fit W(2) ≈ a · W(1) + c · W(0.5) + b. If knowing where the path was at 0.5 in addition to where it is at 1 improved the forecast, c would be nonzero. It is −0.008. Given the present, the past adds nothing.

Why this matters in three pages' time

"Martingale" is the property a fairly priced bet has: its expected future value is its current value. 2.8 will show that under the right probability measure a discounted option price is a martingale, and pricing becomes taking an expectation. The regression slope of 0.9985 above is the first time that word has a number attached.

Q1

W is a Brownian motion. What is the distribution of W(0.5) − W(0.25)?

  • Normal, mean 0, sd 0.5 — variance equals elapsed time: 0.5 − 0.25 = 0.25, sd = √0.25 = 0.5.
  • Normal, mean 0, sd √0.5 − √0.25 ≈ 0.21 — sd subtracts. Standard deviations of independent stretches never add or subtract; variances do. Var over [0.25, 0.5] is 0.5 − 0.25 = 0.25, and the root is taken last.
  • Normal, mean 0, sd 0.25 — sd proportional to time. It is the variance that is proportional to time. sd 0.25 would make the variance 0.0625 and the walk four times too tame.
  • Normal, mean 0, sd √0.5 ≈ 0.71 — forgot to subtract. That is the spread of W(0.5) from the origin. The increment starts at W(0.25), not at 0; only the stretch [0.25, 0.5] contributes.
Q2

At a fixed time t, you measure the secant slope (W(t+h) − W(t))/h for smaller and smaller h. As h → 0 the typical size of the slope:

  • Grows without bound, like 1/√h — the increment has sd √h, so the slope has sd √h/h = 1/√h. W is continuous but has no derivative anywhere.
  • Flattens toward zero — zigzags average out. That is what a smooth function's wobble does. W's wobble is √h-sized at every scale, which is far larger than h; dividing by h makes it blow up, not vanish.
  • Settles to a finite value, the derivative — continuous implies differentiable. Continuity says |ΔW| → 0 (it does: like √h). Differentiability needs |ΔW| ∝ h. √h shrinks far too slowly for a tangent to exist.
  • Stays roughly constant in size, but random — self-similarity means scale-free slope. Self-similarity holds for W(t) scaled by √c, not for the slope. The slope picks up a factor √c on every zoom, so it is the one thing that is not scale-free.
Q3

A Brownian path has reached W(1) = 1.5. Seen from now, W(2) has expected value and standard deviation:

  • 1.5 and 1 — martingale: the expected future value is the current value. Only the increment over [1, 2] is still random, and its variance is 2 − 1 = 1.
  • 1.5 and √2 — unconditional spread. √2 is the sd of W(2) seen from time 0. Standing at time 1 you already know the first unit of variance; conditioning removes it, leaving 2 − 1 = 1.
  • Something below 1.5, and 1 — mean reversion. W has no anchor and no memory of zero. Its expected next increment is zero wherever it is: paths near +1.5 end on average at +1.5.
  • Something above 1.5, and 1 — momentum. A path that rose is not more likely to keep rising. Independent increments: the move over [1, 2] is drawn fresh, mean zero, whatever happened over [0, 1].
Q4

You zoom into the first 1 % of a Brownian path and stretch time by 100 to fill the screen. For the picture to look statistically like the whole path, the vertical axis must be stretched by:

  • ×10 — W(t/100) has variance t/100; multiplying by √100 = 10 restores variance t. {√c·W(t/c)} is again a Brownian motion.
  • ×100 — stretch both axes alike. That stretches space by c where the path only needs √c: the zoomed path would have variance 100 times too large. sd, not variance, is what the vertical axis measures.
  • ×1, no vertical change — zoom only stretches time. The first 1 % of the path only ranges about a tenth as far as the whole. Left at ×1 it looks like a nearly flat line — the "smooth up close" illusion, and it is false.
  • No stretch will work — the zoomed path is visibly smoother — smoothness at small scale. There is no scale at which W is smooth; the √h-sized wobble is there at every h. Rescaled by √c the zoom is indistinguishable from the original.
Q5

Build a walk from ±1 coin flips with √dt scaling, and look at its increments over 250 steps. Their distribution is:

  • Gaussian to within measurement — excess kurtosis −2/250 = −0.008, measured −0.001. Any finite-variance step gives Gaussian increments in the limit; the coin is forgotten. That is why the definition can say "Gaussian" without choosing a coin.
  • Two-valued, ±√(250·dt) — the coin survives. One step is two-valued. A sum of 250 independent steps takes 251 values and is already bell-shaped; the central limit theorem is doing what it does.
  • Uniform between −√(250·dt) and +√(250·dt) — sums spread out flat. Sums pile up in the middle: there are vastly more ways to get 125 heads than 250. Uniform is what you get by ignoring the counting.
  • It depends on the coin — a biased or three-sided coin would give a different shape — distribution sets the shape. With the mean removed and the variance matched, every finite-variance step converges to the same Gaussian. Only the mean and variance of the step survive the limit.
