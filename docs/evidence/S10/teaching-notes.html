What this page is about, and what it is trying to achieve

2.9 derived the price of a call, C = S₀N(d₁) − Ke−rTN(d₂), and met its first derivative with respect to the stock, the delta Δ = ∂C/∂S₀ = N(d₁), which 2.7 had already hedged with. This page differentiates the rest of the formula. A Greek is a partial derivative of the price with respect to one of its inputs, with a name: delta and gamma for the stock, vega for volatility, theta for time, rho for the risk-free rate. Each one answers a trader's question of the form "if this input moves and nothing else does, how much does my book move?" — and the page's job is to say what each one measures, who watches it, how it comes out of the formula, and how to check an implementation of it.

What the page achieves, in order: (1) the five numbers at the base case, each confirmed two independent ways — by bumping the formula and by the oracle — so that "Δ = 0.627, Γ = 0.0095, vega = 37.8, Θ = −9.80, ρ = 44.7" are measurements, not quotations (A, B, F); (2) the shape of each Greek across spot and expiry, which is what the D6 pricer draws and what a trader's intuition is made of (C); (3) 2.7's PDE re-read as a P&L statement, measured step by step on the hedged book: the option's theta is the price of its gamma, and a hedger paid in realised variance and charged in implied (D); (4) the put's Greeks for free, from parity (E).

Why a developer cares: every risk system, every hedger's screen and every test of a pricer is built on these five derivatives. Part 3 implements them (3.1) and studies how to compute them when there is no formula (3.5: bump sizes, Monte Carlo noise, AAD). This page is where the analytic forms are established and where the units are fixed — raw partials, the same ones the golden file and the C++ carry.

B · Analytic against bump-and-revalue

A bump-and-revalue Greek is a finite difference: move the input by h, re-price, divide. It needs no formula for the derivative, only for the price, which is why Part 3 leans on it; the cost is two errors pulling in opposite directions — truncation, from the terms Taylor's series leaves out, which grow with h, and round-off, from subtracting two nearly equal prices, which grows as h shrinks. Here the closed forms exist, so the bump is the check: central differences at the base case and across the 216-point grid (call and put) agree with the analytic Greeks to 10⁻⁷ or better.

D · Theta pays for gamma: the PDE as a P&L statement, step by step

On 2.7 the hedged book — long one call, short Δ shares, the difference in cash at r, rebalanced N times — came out with mean zero and a spread that fell like 1/√N, and the PDE Θ + ½σ²S²Γ + rSΔ − rV = 0 was what the closed form had to satisfy for that to happen. This section takes the same book, the same code, the same 4,000-path setup (μ = 0, σ_i = 0.4) and writes down on every step where the option's change went. 2.5's lemma on a grid says, over one step with the stock moving by δS,

δV = Θ δt + Δ δS + ½Γ (δS)² + remainder,

and the hedge removes the Δ δS term while the cash leg adds (rSΔ − rV) δt. So the hedged book's step is three named pieces plus the remainder, and summed along a path (discounted):

hedged P&L = Σ e−rtΘ δt + Σ e−rt½Γ(δS)² + Σ e−rt(rSΔ − rV) δt + remainder.

Call the three sums the theta cost, the gamma gain and the carry. The PDE, multiplied by δt and summed, is an identity between them and a fourth sum the price assumed for the gamma gain:

theta cost + carry = −Σ e−rt½σi²S²Γ δt — on every path, to 10⁻¹³.

That is the sentence "theta pays for gamma" with the units attached: what the holder pays in theta (net of carry) is exactly what the formula assumed the curvature would earn at the implied volatility. Whether the curvature does earn it is the one thing the formula cannot assume — it depends on how the stock actually moves — and subtracting the identity from the hedged P&L leaves 2.7's gamma P&L row, Σ e−rt½ΓS²((δS/S)² − σi²δt), plus the remainder. Three things to read off. First, at σ_r = σ_i the gamma gain (7.58 ± 0.05) is what theta and the carry cost (9.71 − 2.14 = 7.57): zero net, within 0.003. The curvature earned exactly what it was priced to earn, because the stock moved with the variance the price assumed. Second, at σ_r = 0.3 and 0.5 the gamma gain moves (4.89, 10.34) and the assumed gain does not move with it (8.69, 6.62 — it changes only because the paths visit different places); the hedged P&L is their difference, −3.80 and +3.72, which is (σ_r² − σ_i²) times the gamma integral to the s.e. — 2.7's Decision 3 result, seen per step, and its own numbers −3.76 / +3.74 sit within 1.5 combined s.e. of this run's on fresh paths. Who watches it: a delta-hedged book is a bet that realised variance will exceed implied, paid for daily in theta. That is what "long gamma, short theta" means on a desk, and 2.11 is where the model's one σ meets a market with a different one for every strike.

E · The put's Greeks, from parity

2.9's parity, C − P = S₀e−qT − Ke−rT, is an identity in every input, so it can be differentiated: the put's Greek is the call's minus the forward's. Why — the derivation, after the numbers

Ten rungs. One identity (rung 2) turns five differentiations into five one-liners; everything else is reading. Notation as the course has it: N(·) the cumulative normal, φ(z) = e^(−z²/2)/√(2π) its density, d₁ and d₂ as 2.9 defined them, ∂ throughout, drift first.

1

What a Greek is. The price is a function of five inputs, C(S₀, K, r, σ, T) — K is fixed by the contract, so four can move. A Greek is the partial derivative with respect to one of them, every other input held still, and it answers "how much does the price move per unit move in this input, to first order." First order is the limitation: A's 0.6321 against Δ = 0.6274 is the gap, and gamma is its name.

2

The identity that does all the work. Write out S₀φ(d₁) and Ke−rTφ(d₂) with d₂ = d₁ − σ√T: the exponents differ by −½d₁² + ½(d₁ − σ√T)² = −d₁σ√T + ½σ²T = −ln(S₀/K) − rT, which is exactly the log of Ke−rT/S₀. So

S₀ φ(d₁) = K e−rT φ(d₂).

Numerically 100 × 0.3784 = 95.12 × 0.3978 = 37.84 — and 37.84 is vega. Keep this: whenever an input moves d₁ and d₂ together (and every input except K moves them by the same amount, since they differ by σ√T), the two φ-terms the chain rule produces cancel.

3

Delta, and why it is only N(d₁). Differentiate C with respect to S₀: the product rule on S₀N(d₁) gives N(d₁) + S₀φ(d₁)·∂d₁/∂S₀; the strike piece gives −Ke−rTφ(d₂)·∂d₂/∂S₀. Both ∂d/∂S₀ are 1/(S₀σ√T), so by rung 2 the two φ-terms cancel and Δ = N(d₁). 2.9 measured it as a bump and as a probability; here it is the first case of the pattern.

4

Gamma. Differentiate Δ = N(d₁) once more: Γ = φ(d₁)·∂d₁/∂S₀ = φ(d₁)/(S₀σ√T) = 0.3784/(100 × 0.4) = 0.00946. Always positive — the call is convex in S — and shaped like the normal density in ln S, which is why C's peak sits where d₁ = −σ√T, at Ke−(r + 3σ²/2)T, and moves to the strike as T → 0 while its height grows like 1/√T: the step in Δ is getting sharper.

5

Vega. Differentiate with respect to σ: the chain rule gives S₀φ(d₁)·∂d₁/∂σ − Ke−rTφ(d₂)·∂d₂/∂σ. Now ∂d₂/∂σ = ∂d₁/∂σ − √T, so by rung 2 what survives is Ke−rTφ(d₂)√T = S₀φ(d₁)√T = 37.84. Positive for the call and (rung 9) equal for the put: more volatility is worth more to anyone long an option, whichever way it is struck — vega has no direction in it (P4a). Grows with √T; vanishes at expiry.

6

Rho. Differentiate with respect to r: the chain rule gives the two φ-terms with ∂d₁/∂r = ∂d₂/∂r = √T/σ — equal, so by rung 2 they cancel — plus the derivative of the discount factor, +KTe−rTN(d₂) = 44.72. So P5: r does enter through d₁ and d₂, and that route contributes nothing; ρ is the strike piece's derivative alone, and it is positive because paying K later is cheaper when rates are higher. The put's is negative (rung 9).

7

Theta. The price depends on t only through τ = T − t, so Θ = ∂V/∂t = −∂C/∂T. Differentiating with respect to T: the discount factor gives +rKe−rTN(d₂), and the φ-terms, with ∂d₂/∂T = ∂d₁/∂T − σ/(2√T), leave Ke−rTφ(d₂)·σ/(2√T) = S₀φ(d₁)σ/(2√T). Flip the sign:

Θ = −S₀φ(d₁)σ/(2√T) − rKe−rTN(d₂) = −7.568 − 2.236 = −9.804.

The first term is −½σ²S₀²Γ — check: ½ × 0.16 × 10⁴ × 0.00946 = 7.57 — the gamma term; the second is the carry on the strike. Deep in the money φ(d₁) → 0 and only the carry is left, −rKe^(−rT) = −4.76: a forward's theta (P3a). Deep out both vanish.

8

The PDE, re-read. Put rung 7 beside 2.7's Θ + ½σ²S²Γ + rSΔ − rV = 0: it is the same equation, because rSΔ − rV = rS₀N(d₁) − rC = rKe−rTN(d₂). 2.7 derived the PDE from the hedge and checked the formula against it; here the formula's theta is computed directly and the PDE is what it says. Read as money per unit time on the hedged book: −Θ = ½σ²S²Γ + (rSΔ − rV) — what you pay in theta is what the curvature is expected to earn at the implied σ, plus the carry. D measured each term along 4,000 paths: 9.71 = 7.57 + 2.14.

9

The put. Differentiate parity, P = C − S₀ + Ke−rT: Δ_P = Δ_C − 1, Γ and vega unchanged (the forward has neither), Θ_P = Θ_C + rKe^(−rT), ρ_P = ρ_C − KTe^(−rT). E. Nothing is re-derived; the forward's Greeks are what a linear instrument has, and they can be read off 2.7's control.

10

Units, and the two cousins. Everything above is a raw partial: per 1.00 of σ, per year, per 1.00 of r. A desk quotes vega per vol point (÷100: 0.378), theta per day (÷365: −0.027), rho per basis point (÷10⁴: 0.0045), and the scaling belongs at the display, once, where everyone can see it — the oracle, the C++ and the TypeScript must agree on the raw number or no test means anything (the T0 decision). The dividend-rho ∂V/∂q = −TS₀e^(−qT)N(d₁) is the stock piece's T-weight as ρ is the strike piece's; the oracle carries it, the page does not name it among the five. The second-order cousins traders also watch — vanna ∂Δ/∂σ, volga ∂²V/∂σ², charm ∂Δ/∂t — are not on this page; they are where 2.11's smile makes them matter.

Checks Q1–Q6 (I5) — approved 2026-11-08

One per section; every wrong option names its misconception. Q2, Q3, Q4 and Q6's distractors are your P2, P3 and P5 misses; the rest are the handover's listening list. As committed in drafts/2.10-checks.md (m/L2 e35a987); Q3's feedback there also carries the measured 7.58 against 9.71 − 2.14.

Q1. Δ = 0.627 here. The stock moves from 100 to 101 and the call price moves by:
  1. ✓ About 0.632 — slightly more than Δ, because the second-order term ½Γ(δS)² = 0.0047 is positive — 18.023 → 18.655; and down to 99 the fall is 0.623, less than Δ, for the same reason.
  2. ✗ Exactly 0.627 — delta is the price change per unit of stock — first order mistaken for the whole; Δ is a derivative, not a difference, and it has itself moved to 0.637 by the time the stock reaches 101.
  3. ✗ About 0.632 up but 0.632 down too — the curvature cancels on the way down — gamma's sign misread; Γ > 0 helps both ways: more on the way up, less on the way down.
  4. ✗ 0.627 × 18.023 — delta is a percentage of the price — a unit error; Δ is per unit of stock, in price units per price unit, and 0.627 of a share hedges one option.
Q2. With a year to run (S₀ = K = 100, σ = 0.4), gamma is largest:
  1. ✓ Below the strike, near S = 75 — Γ = φ(d₁)/(Sσ√T) peaks where d₁ = −σ√T, at Ke^(−(r + 3σ²/2)T) — 0.0123 there against 0.0095 at the strike; the peak moves to the strike only as T → 0.
  2. ✗ At the strike, always — the short-expiry picture generalised; true at τ = 0.05 (peak 98.6), not at τ = 1.
  3. ✗ Deep in the money, where the option is worth most — value confused with curvature; deep in the money the call is a forward, Δ = 1 and Γ = 0.
  4. ✗ It is the same everywhere — gamma is a constant like σ — a parameter mistaken for a function; Γ is a function of (t, S), and that is why the hedge is rebalanced.
Q3. A hedger is long one call and short Δ shares. The stock moves by δS in either direction, and nothing else happens. The hedged book:
  1. ✓ Gains ½Γ(δS)², whichever way the stock went — the book is long gamma — and pays for that through theta while nothing moves — on average the two cancel (the PDE); on any one step they do not.
  2. ✗ Neither gains nor loses — that is what hedged means — the expectation mistaken for the realisation; the delta term is removed, the gamma term is not, and the measured gamma gain is 7.58 on the year.
  3. ✗ Gains if the stock rose and loses if it fell — that is the unhedged option; the hedge removes direction and leaves curvature, which has no sign.
  4. ✗ Loses, because the hedge is stale and must be re-set — re-hedging confused with P&L; resetting Δ costs nothing in itself and does not remove the ½Γ(δS)² already earned.
Q4. Theta for a one-year call at σ = 0.4 is −9.80 per year at the money. It is most negative:
  1. ✓ Near the strike, where gamma is large — Θ = −½σ²S²Γ − rKe^(−rT)N(d₂), and the first term is −7.57 of the −9.80 — the trough is −10.20 at S = 114, a little above the strike because of the carry term.
  2. ✗ Deep in the money, where there is the most value to lose — value confused with decay; deep in the money the call is a forward and its theta is the carry alone, −rKe^(−rT) = −4.76.
  3. ✗ Deep out of the money, where the option is all time value — "time value" misread; far from the strike φ(d₁) → 0 and so does theta: there is nothing left to decay.
  4. ✗ It is −9.80 everywhere — theta is the option's decay rate — a point value mistaken for a constant; Θ is a function of S, like Γ.
Q5. Vega = S₀φ(d₁)√T = 37.8 per unit of σ here. As expiry T grows, vega at the money:
  1. ✓ Grows, like √T — 27.5 at T = ½, 37.8 at 1, 50.8 at 2 — more time is more that volatility can do; vega vanishes at expiry.
  2. ✗ Shrinks — a longer option is less sensitive to anything — the direction reversed; it is gamma and theta that shrink with T, like 1/√T.
  3. ✗ Depends on whether the stock is expected to rise — drift smuggled back in; vega is ∂C/∂σ, a sensitivity to the size of moves, not their direction, and μ is not in the formula.
  4. ✗ 37.8 means a 1 % rise in σ moves the price by 37.8 — a unit error; 37.8 is per 1.00 of σ, so one vol point is 0.378 — measured 0.3783.
Q6. ρ = ∂C/∂r for the call. In C = S₀N(d₁) − Ke^(−rT)N(d₂), r appears in the discount factor and inside d₁ and d₂. Rho is:
  1. ✓ +KTe^(−rT)N(d₂) = 44.7: the discount factor's derivative alone, because the two d-terms cancel through S₀φ(d₁) = Ke^(−rT)φ(d₂) — positive: a higher rate makes the strike cheaper to pay; the put's is −50.4.
  2. ✗ Negative for the call — a higher rate discounts the payoff more — the stock piece forgotten; the discount hits the strike you pay, not the stock you receive, and the forward rises with r.
  3. ✗ The sum of a discount effect and a drift effect through d₁, d₂, both non-zero — the cancellation missed; the d-terms are there in the differentiation and gone in the result, exactly as in Δ = N(d₁).
  4. ✗ Zero — the hedge removes the rate as it removes the drift — r confused with μ; the hedge removes the stock's expected return, and r is what the hedged book earns.
