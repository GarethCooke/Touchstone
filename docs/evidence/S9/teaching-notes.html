What this page is about, and what it is trying to achieve

2.8 ended with the price written as an expectation, V = e−rT·EQ[max(ST − K, 0)], and a Monte Carlo pricer that estimates it with an error bar. This page does that expectation by hand. Under Q the terminal price is lognormal, so the expectation is an ordinary integral. The integral splits in two at the strike — the stock you receive on exercise, the strike you pay — and after one substitution each piece is a normal probability. The result is the formula 2.7 checked against the PDE and 2.8 borrowed as its benchmark: C = S₀N(d₁) − Ke−rTN(d₂). Nothing new is priced and no assumption is added; the only new symbols are the two thresholds, d₂ and d₁, and they appear because the algebra produces them, not before.

What the page achieves, in order: (1) the Monte Carlo pricer, the integral and the closed form are three views of one number, 18.023; (2) N(d₂) and N(d₁) become two measurable quantities (0.470 and 0.627) instead of two symbols — the probability of exercise under Q, and the share of the stock's forward value that comes from the paths that exercise, which is also the delta 2.7 hedged with; (3) the put comes free, and put–call parity needs no model at all; (4) the formula's limits (σ → 0, T → 0, S₀ → ∞, S₀ → 0) are the sanity checks a developer runs on any pricer; (5) a dividend yield and Black-76 are the same formula re-spelled, not new formulas; and (6) the formula is checked against the oracle, QuantLib, at 7,234 committed points — the first time the oracle appears on a study page.

Why a developer cares: this is the function every pricer, Greeks calculator, implied-volatility solver and test oracle in Part 3 calls. Knowing where each symbol comes from is what makes it testable; 2.10 differentiates it and Part 3 implements it.

B · What N(d₁) and N(d₂) mean

Split the payoff at the strike: the call pays ST − K when ST > K and nothing otherwise. Writing 1{ST > K} for the indicator of exercise (1 on those paths, 0 on the rest),

C = e−rT·EQ[ST·1{ST > K}] − K·e−rT·EQ[1{ST > K}].

Two pieces: the stock received on exercise, and the strike paid on exercise, each counted only on the paths where exercise happens. The second expectation is a probability, Q(ST > K). The first is the stock's forward value restricted to those paths. C · The put, and why parity needs no model

The put's integral runs over the other side of the strike and gives P = Ke−rTN(−d₂) − S₀N(−d₁) = 13.146. But the put does not need its own integral. On every path, max(ST − K, 0) − max(K − ST, 0) = ST − K: the call and the put differ by the forward's payoff, path by path, whatever the model. Discount the Q-expectation of both sides and the only model fact used is EQ[ST] = S₀erT, the discounted stock being a Q-martingale, which 2.8 measured and which is no-arbitrage, not lognormality.

D · The edges: four limits as convergence tables

E · A dividend yield, and Black-76: three spellings of one formula

With a continuous dividend yield q the holder of the stock collects dividends and the holder of the option does not. Under Q the total return of the stock — price plus dividends — must be r, so the price alone drifts at r − q: ST = S₀e(r − q − ½σ²)T + σ√T·Z, 2.8's simulator with one more parameter. Every step of the derivation goes through unchanged and the result is your P5: S₀ becomes S₀e−qT in the stock piece and r becomes r − q inside d₁ and d₂. The call gets cheaper — value leaks to the holder of the stock, who is not the holder of the option — and the put dearer; across the whole grid, without exception.

The third spelling notices that S₀e−qT·erT = S₀e(r − q)T = EQ[ST] = F, the forward price, and writes the formula in F:

C = e−rT·[F·N(d₁) − K·N(d₂)], d₁ = (ln(F/K) + ½σ²T)/(σ√T), d₂ = d₁ − σ√T.

That is Black-76. r has left d₁ and survives only in the discount; q has disappeared entirely, folded into F. F is whatever the market quotes for delivery at T — a futures price, an FX forward, a forward swap rate — which is why one formula prices options on forwards and futures across asset classes. The same number comes out of all three spellings to 10⁻¹², and out of the simulator within its error bar.

Why — the derivation, after the numbers

Eleven rungs. Each one is a line of algebra and the number the experiment measured for it; the symbols d₂ and d₁ appear where the algebra makes them, in rungs 4 and 5, and not before. Notation as the course has it: drift first, N(·) for the cumulative normal from this page on (Φ on 2.6 only), Z a standard normal, φ(z) its density.

1

Start where 2.8 stopped. The price is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of the payoff:

C = e−rT·EQ[max(ST − K, 0)].

2.8 estimated this by drawing paths; the question is whether it can be evaluated exactly.

2

ST under Q is lognormal — 2.6's exact draw with r in the drift slot:

ST = S₀·e(r − ½σ²)T + σ√T·Z, Z ~ N(0, 1).

So the expectation is an integral over one standard normal: EQ[g(ST)] = ∫ g(S(z))·φ(z) dz. (In S it is the same integral against the lognormal density; the experiment does it both ways.)

3

Split at the strike. max(ST − K, 0) = (ST − K)·1{ST > K}, so

C = e−rT·EQ[ST·1{ST > K}] − K·e−rT·Q(ST > K).

The stock received on exercise, and the strike paid on exercise, each counted only where exercise happens. Two pieces; the rest of the derivation evaluates them one at a time.

4

The threshold, and d₂ is born. When does the path exercise? ST > K is a condition on z:

(r − ½σ²)T + σ√T·z > ln(K/S₀) ⟺ z > −d₂, d₂ := (ln(S₀/K) + (r − ½σ²)T)/(σ√T).

d₂ is nothing but the exercise threshold measured in standard deviations of Z, with the sign chosen so that a larger d₂ means more exercise. Since Q(Z > −d₂) = Q(Z < d₂) by symmetry, Q(ST > K) = N(d₂). Here ln(S₀/K) = 0, (r − ½σ²)T = 0.05 − 0.08 = −0.03, σ√T = 0.4: d₂ = −0.075 and N(d₂) = 0.4701; measured 0.4707 ± 0.0005. The strike piece is K·e−rT·N(d₂) = 95.123 × 0.4701 = 44.718; measured 44.77 ± 0.05.

5

The stock piece, and d₁ is born. On the exercising paths the stock is S₀e(r − ½σ²)T·eσ√T·z, so

EQ[ST·1{ST > K}] = S₀·e(r − ½σ²)T·∫−d₂∞ eσ√T·z·φ(z) dz.

Complete the square in the exponent: eσ√T·z·e−½z² = e½σ²T·e−½(z − σ√T)². The integrand is e½σ²T times a normal density centred at σ√T instead of 0, so the integral is e½σ²T·P(Z > −d₂ − σ√T) = e½σ²T·N(d₂ + σ√T). Name the shifted threshold:

d₁ := d₂ + σ√T = (ln(S₀/K) + (r + ½σ²)T)/(σ√T).

The −½σ²T and the +½σ²T cancel, leaving EQ[ST·1{ST > K}] = S₀erTN(d₁); discounted, the stock piece is S₀N(d₁) = 62.741, measured 62.79 ± 0.07. Here d₁ = −0.075 + 0.4 = 0.325, N(d₁) = 0.6274.

6

Assemble.

C = S₀N(d₁) − Ke−rTN(d₂) = 62.741 − 44.718 = 18.023.

That is the Black–Scholes formula, and it is three views of one number: the quadrature of rung 2's integral in S and in z returns 18.0229514502 to 10⁻¹⁴, and the Q-run 18.011 ± 0.031. 2.7 checked that this function satisfies the PDE; 2.8 borrowed it; it is now derived.

7

What the shift means — why N(d₁) is a probability. The factor that completed the square, eσ√T·z − ½σ²T, is ST/EQ[ST]: 2.5's exponential martingale with a = σ, mean 1, and exactly 2.8's Girsanov weight with λ = −σ. Rung 5 says that weighting each path by ST/EQ[ST] shifts Z by σ√T — the same fact 2.8 measured as "adding a drift is reweighting the paths", read in the other direction. Under that weighting the probability of exercise is N(d₂ + σ√T) = N(d₁). So:

  • N(d₂) is the probability of exercise under Q — paths counted equally;
  • N(d₁) is the probability of exercise under the measure that weights each path by the stock it delivers — your "how much of the stock's value comes from the paths that finish in the money", EQ[ST·1{ST > K}]/EQ[ST] = 0.6274, measured 0.6278 ± 0.0005.

The weighting favours the high paths, which all exercise, so N(d₁) > N(d₂) always. N(d₁) is also the delta: dC/dS₀ = N(d₁) + S₀φ(d₁)·∂d₁/∂S₀ − Ke−rTφ(d₂)·∂d₂/∂S₀, and the last two terms cancel because S₀φ(d₁) = Ke−rTφ(d₂) — the same completed square as rung 5. The bump gives 0.62741; 2.7 hedged with 0.63 as a black box. And the number "the average stock price on the exercising paths" names, EQ[ST | ST > K] = S₀erTN(d₁)/N(d₂) = 140.3 (measured 140.23 ± 0.06), is a different quantity that appears nowhere in the price.

8

The put. The same integral over z < −d₂, or N(−x) = 1 − N(x) applied to both pieces:

P = Ke−rTN(−d₂) − S₀N(−d₁) = 50.405 − 37.259 = 13.146; measured 13.122 ± 0.017.
9

Parity, from no-arbitrage alone. Path by path, max(ST − K, 0) − max(K − ST, 0) = ST − K (max |difference| on 10⁶ draws: 0). Take e−rTEQ of both sides: C − P = e−rTEQ[ST] − Ke−rT = S₀ − Ke−rT = 4.877, using only that the discounted stock is a Q-martingale (2.8). Nothing lognormal was used; on the formula the same identity falls out of N(d) + N(−d) = 1. Measured 4.889 ± 0.042, the forward's noise.

10

A dividend yield, and Black-76. With a continuous yield q the stock's price drifts at r − q under Q (its total return, price plus dividends, is r), so rung 2 reads ST = S₀e(r − q − ½σ²)T + σ√T·Z and rungs 3–6 go through with S₀ → S₀e−qT in the stock piece and r → r − q in d₁, d₂ (your P5). Writing F = S₀e(r−q)T = EQ[ST] gives the third spelling,

C = e−rT[F·N(d₁) − K·N(d₂)], d₁ = (ln(F/K) + ½σ²T)/(σ√T),

Black-76: r survives only in the discount, q has vanished into F, and F can be any quoted forward or futures price. At q = 0.03 the three spellings give 16.210727 to 10⁻¹², the simulator 16.23 ± 0.03, and the call is cheaper than at q = 0 by 1.81; E.

11

The edges as checks. σ → 0 collapses ST onto the forward and the call onto max(S₀ − Ke−rT, 0); T → 0 onto the intrinsic value; S₀ → ∞ sends both N's to 1 and the call to the forward; S₀ → 0 sends everything to 0. At the two kinks the convergence slows to linear in σ and to √T, with the slopes D measured. A pricer that fails any of these is wrong before it is imprecise.
