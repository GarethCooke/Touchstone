The statement, and where it comes from

2Taylor to second order, then 2.3's rulebook

First — what problem it solves

You have a model for how one thing moves at random — the price, S — and you hold something that is a function of it: an option worth V(t, S), a hedged book, log S, e^(W). The question every later page asks is "how does my thing move, given how S moves?" — what is its drift, and how much of S's noise does it carry? For a smooth S the chain rule answers that. For a Brownian S the chain rule gives the wrong drift, by exactly ½g″ × (ΔW)² per step — ΔW the step's random increment, whose square is dt-sized and never dies (2.3) — and the lemma is the chain rule with that term put back. It is the only tool on the course that turns "S moves like this" into "f(t, S) moves like this", and everything from here is an application of it:

2.6 — solve dS = μS dt + σS dW by asking how log S moves: d(log S) = (μ − ½σ²) dt + σ dW, a plain drift plus Brownian motion, so S_T = S₀·e^((μ − ½σ²)T + σW_T) in closed form — that is how prices are simulated, and the −½σ² is §5's E[e^W] = e^(t/2) in a suit. 2.7 — hold V − Δ·S; the lemma gives dV, choosing Δ = ∂V/∂S cancels the dW term, and what is left must earn the riskless rate: the Black–Scholes PDE, in which the lemma's ½b²f_xx is the ½σ²S²·Γ term — gamma costing theta. 2.8 — change the drift of S and the lemma says the noise coefficient of V, and so the hedge, does not change. Without the lemma you cannot write dV at all, so no delta, no PDE, no price.

It is also the course's thesis in one line. A forward is linear in S, g″ = 0, the extra term is zero and the chain rule was right all along — linear products need no stochastic calculus. An option is curved, g″ ≠ 0, and the extra term is what the curvature earns or pays from the price's jitter. §5's e^(W) is the smallest curved payoff there is, and its ½e^W dt is that earning.

2.3's Taylor expansion, one step at a time, says how much a smooth g changes when its argument moves from Wᵢ to Wᵢ + ΔWᵢ:

g(Wᵢ + ΔWᵢ) − g(Wᵢ) = g′(Wᵢ)·ΔWᵢ + ½g″(Wᵢ)·(ΔWᵢ)² + ⅙g‴(Wᵢ)·(ΔWᵢ)³ + …
one step. Nothing stochastic yet: this is calculus about the function g, true for any number ΔWᵢ. The ½ and the ⅙ are Taylor's coefficients, 1/2! and 1/3!

Add the n steps. The left side telescopes to g(W_T) − g(0), exactly as W_T² did in 2.4 (aside below). The right side becomes three sums:

g(W_T) − g(0) = Σ g′(Wᵢ)·ΔWᵢ + ½Σ g″(Wᵢ)·(ΔWᵢ)² + [third order and beyond]
the (ΔW)²-form. Still exact algebra on every grid, for every path, for every g — it is Taylor's theorem summed. For g = x² the red bracket is empty (g‴ = 0) and this is the identity you wrote on 2.3 ("Why ordinary calculus fails"): W_T² = 2Σ W·ΔW + Σ(ΔW)² — which 2.4 then solves for the left sum, Σ W(tᵢ)·ΔWᵢ = ½W_T² − ½Σ(ΔW)². The JSON checks it to 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ (§3)
g(W_T) − g(0) ≈ ½Σ g″(Wᵢ)·dt + Σ g′(Wᵢ)·ΔWᵢ
the dt-form: the same line with two things done to it — (ΔWᵢ)² replaced by dt (the rulebook, 2.3) and the red bracket dropped (P5). Both are limits, not identities: on a grid this line is off by a noise that shrinks like 1/√n (§3, §4). The blue sum is a left-endpoint sum, so it tends to the Itô integral ∫₀ᵀ g′(W) dW (2.4); the orange sum is an ordinary Riemann sum, so it tends to ½∫₀ᵀ g″(W) dt
dg(W_t) = ½g″(W_t) dt + g′(W_t) dW_t
Itô's lemma, in the d-notation of 2.3's rulebook: the same statement written for one infinitesimal step. Read it as "over the next dt, g moves by half its curvature times dt, plus its slope times the kick". Order of terms, from here to the end of the course: the certain dt part first, the random dW part second — a dt + b dW — as the index and 2.6's dS = μS dt + σS dW have it. (2.4's "Into 2.5" paragraph wrote the two the other way round; it is the one place to flip.) The chain rule is the blue term alone. The orange term is 2.4's −½T with g″ in place of 2 — what 2.3 called the survivor

Two forms, and it matters which is which. The (ΔW)²-form is an equals sign: it holds at n = 3 with made-up numbers, no Brownian motion required. The dt-form is an arrow: it needs 2.3 twice — Σ(ΔW)² → T for the orange term, Σ|ΔW|³ → 0 for the red bracket. The page's argument is the same as 2.4's: exact identity, then one limit. This time the identity has a name.

Aside — the telescoping, for a general g

Write gᵢ for g(Wᵢ). The one-step left side on step i is gᵢ₊₁ − gᵢ, and consecutive differences collapse to last minus first. For n = 3:

step 0:  g(W₀ + ΔW₀) − g(W₀)  =  g₁ − g₀
step 1:  g(W₁ + ΔW₁) − g(W₁)  =  g₂ − g₁
step 2:  g(W₂ + ΔW₂) − g(W₂)  =  g₃ − g₂
                                 ──────────
sum:                             g₃ − g₀  =  g(W_T) − g(0)

g₁ enters step 0 with a plus and step 1 with a minus; g₂ likewise. Only the ends survive, and no limit is taken. This is why the left side of the lemma is one number per path — g at the end minus g at the start — while the right side is a sum over the whole path. For g = x², g(0) = 0 and the left side is W_T², as in 2.4; for g = eˣ, g(0) = 1 and the left side is e^(W_T) − 1; for g = x³ it is W_T³.

Aside — why the ½ is there and why the (ΔW)² term survives at all

The ½ is Taylor's: the second-order coefficient of any smooth function is g″/2! (for g = x²: (W + Δ)² = W² + 2WΔ + Δ², and ½g″ = ½·2 = 1 is the coefficient of Δ² — check). It is the same ½ as 2.4's −½T, which is why the −½T is "2.4's number" and not a new one.

Why the term survives summation is 2.3's whole point, restated with g″ in front. Each (ΔWᵢ)² is dt-sized on average (E[(ΔW)²] = dt), so ½Σ g″(Wᵢ)(ΔWᵢ)² is n terms of size dt: a sum of size T that does not go away. For a smooth x, each (Δxᵢ)² is dt²-sized, n of them make dt, and the term vanishes (§6). The third-order term is n pieces of size dt^(3/2), which sum to something of size √dt → 0 (§4). "Size dt survives, anything smaller dies" — the rulebook — is the sorting rule, and the second-order term is the only one that lands exactly on the line.

Aside — P1 and P2: why ½ and not √t

"W_t is about √t in size" is true — sd(W_t) = √t — and it is the wrong thing to exponentiate. Take the one-step coin version: W_t is +√t or −√t with equal chance. Then E[e^(W_t)] = ½e^(√t) + ½e^(−√t), and Taylor each piece:

up:     e^(+√t) − 1  =  +√t  +  ½t  +  ⅙t^(3/2)  + …
down:   e^(−√t) − 1  =  −√t  +  ½t  −  ⅙t^(3/2)  + …
                       ─────────────────────────────
average:                  0   +  ½t  +     0      + …   =  ½t + (smaller)

The √t parts cancel between up and down — that is the Itô sum having mean zero. The ½t parts add — that is ½g″·(ΔW)² with g″ = 1 at W = 0 and (ΔW)² = t. So E[e^(W_t)] − 1 ≈ ½t, not √t: the up move gains more than the down move loses, by exactly the curvature of eˣ, and the excess is second order in the step. Over many small steps the ½t per step compounds to e^(t/2). The size of W_t sets the spread of e^(W_t); the mean is set by the curvature acting on the spread squared, which is t. This is the same fact as "mean of a lognormal = e^(μ + ½σ²)", and it is the −½σ² that 2.6 is built on.

For P2 the same arithmetic gives the constant: the mean grows by ½t per unit of Var(W_t) = t, so subtracting ½t from the exponent takes it out exactly, and e^(W_t − ½t) stays at 1. Check it with the lemma on a function of two variables — f(t, x) = e^(x − ½t), which has a t in it as well as an x. Itô for f(t, W_t) picks up the ordinary time derivative on top of the two terms above: df = (f_t + ½f_xx) dt + f_x dW. Here f_t = −½f, f_x = f, f_xx = f, so df = (−½f + ½f) dt + f dW = f dW. No dt term at all: the −½t was chosen to cancel the ½f_xx exactly, and what is left is an Itô integral — a martingale.

Aside — from dg(W) to the index's general form, one addition at a time

The index writes the lemma for a function f(t, X) of time and of a process X that has its own drift and volatility. That is three things added to §2's line, and each is one step. Glossary first. dX = a dt + b dW means: over one step of length dt, X changes by a·dt + b·ΔW. The a·dt part happens for certain and is the same every step — a is the drift, the rate X moves at on average, per unit time (E[ΔX] = a·dt; units of X per unit time). The b·ΔW part is the random part: ΔW is the √dt-sized, mean-zero Brownian increment (the S1 ledger's "kick"), and b scales it — b is the volatility, or diffusion coefficient: the size of X's random step per √time (sd(ΔX) = b·√dt). Q5

x(t) is a smooth curve (say t²) and g is smooth. Which rule does g(x(t)) obey?

  • dg = g′(x) dx — the chain rule, no second-order term — each (Δx)² is dt²-sized, so ½Σ g″(xᵢ)(Δxᵢ)² is dt-sized and vanishes: 1.26/n for eˣ on t², measured to five figures.
  • dg = ½g″(x) dt + g′(x) dx, as for W — Itô's term taken as a general correction to calculus. It is the price of Σ(ΔW)² = T. For x = t² that sum is 4/(3n) → 0 and the term is an error of exactly ½∫₀¹ g″(x(t)) dt — 0.7313 for eˣ — that is the same at n = 10⁶ as at n = 100.
  • dg = g′(x) dx + ½g″(x)(dx)², and the second term matters — Taylor's second-order term mistaken for a surviving one. It is there on every grid, exactly, and it sums to 1.26/n: gone in the limit. The question is never whether Taylor has the term, it is whether Σ(Δ·)² survives.
  • It depends on g: some functions pick up the dt term, some do not — the path's property attributed to the function. The criterion is the quadratic variation of what g is applied to. For W every g with g″ ≠ 0 gets the term (x², eˣ, x³ all did); for t² none of them does (same three, §6).
