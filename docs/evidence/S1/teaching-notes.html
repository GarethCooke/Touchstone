Step 2 · the object and the three facts

2The walk, and everything 2.1 borrows from probability

The object is as small as it looks. Flip a fair coin; step \(\xi_i = +1\) or \(-1\); your position after \(n\) steps is the sum

$$S_n = \xi_1 + \xi_2 + \dots + \xi_n .$$

Each step has mean \(\mathbb{E}[\xi_i] = 0\) and variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\xi_i) = \mathbb{E}[\xi_i^2] = 1\). This page uses exactly three facts about randomness, no more:

F1Expectations add, always: \(\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]\).
F2Variances add for independent variables: \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)\).
F3Standard deviation is the square root of variance — it carries the units of the thing itself.

F2 is the load-bearing one, so here is its two-line proof for zero-mean \(X, Y\) (which is all we need; means shift nothing). Expand the square and take expectations with F1:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X+Y)^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] + 2\,\mathbb{E}[XY] + \mathbb{E}[Y^2],$$

and independence makes the cross term factor: \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0\). What's left is \(\operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y)\). That's the whole proof — independence is what kills the cross term, and killing cross terms is what this entire subject runs on.

Applied \(n\) times to the walk:

$$\mathbb{E}[S_n] = 0 \quad\text{(exactly)}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n, \qquad \operatorname{sd}(S_n) = \sqrt{n}.$$
Step 3 · prediction 1 resolved

3Zero mean is not zero distance

Your P1 contains a true statement: \(\mathbb{E}[S_n] = 0\), exactly, at every \(n\). The walk has no drift and never develops one. But that expectation averages over worlds, not over a world: for every walk that ends at +80 there's an equally likely mirror ending at −80, and they cancel in the average while both end 80 away. The number that answers "how far, typically?" is not the mean of \(S_n\) but its typical size — the standard deviation, \(\sqrt{n}\) — and for the mean of \(|S_n|\) itself the experiment measures

$$\mathbb{E}\,|S_n| \;\approx\; 0.80\,\sqrt{n} \qquad\text{(the exact asymptote is } \sqrt{2/\pi} = 0.7979\ldots\text{, } \S5).$$

The bars carry all the weight there: \(\mathbb{E}[S_n]\) is the average position (exactly 0, forever); \(\mathbb{E}\,|S_n|\) is the average distance (growing like √n, forever). One easy-to-miss glyph, two different animals — worth protecting the reader from when you write this up.

So after 10,000 steps: sd \(=100\), mean distance \(\approx 80\). Not zero — and not 5,000 either. Cancellation is real but imperfect, and the leftover grows like \(\sqrt{n}\), forever.

Step 4 · prediction 2 resolved

4Variance adds; standard deviation doesn't

P2 asked what 4× the time does. Variance is the thing that adds (F2): four times the steps means four times the variance, always. But distance lives in sd units, and the square root undoes the linearity:

$$\operatorname{Var}(S_{4n}) = 4n \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{sd}(S_{4n}) = 2\sqrt{n}.$$

Quadruple the time, double the typical distance. Not "about the same": the spread never stops growing — your last column shows E|Sₙ|/n falling from 0.081 to 0.0025 across ×1,000 more steps — a factor of \(\sqrt{1000} \approx 32\), which is the index's phrasing "mean absolute distance does not grow linearly" made numerical. And not 4× either: sds don't add. \(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} \ne \sqrt{a+b}\) — writing it out, \((\sqrt a + \sqrt b)^2 = a + b + 2\sqrt{ab}\), which double-counts co-movement that independent stretches don't have.

This is why the maths runs its bookkeeping in variance and only converts to sd at the end, and it is the first useful desk fact of the course: independent risks combine in quadrature.

Step 5 · two remarks before the punchline

5A rigid rule under the noise — and the coin doesn't matter

For the curious — where E|Sₙ| ≈ 0.80√n comes from

At large \(n\), \(S_n/\sqrt{n}\) behaves like a standard Gaussian \(Z\) (that's 2.2's business — here we only borrow the constant), and \(\mathbb{E}|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.7979\). Your table's 0.804–0.810 sits just above it — the gap is finite-n effects plus a ±1% sampling band, and the check only asks for 3%.

Second remark, and hold onto it for 2.3. Watch the squared position for one step, using F1 and independence:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{n+1}^2\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(S_n + \xi_{n+1})^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[S_n^2] + 2\,\mathbb{E}[S_n]\,\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n+1}] + \mathbb{E}[\xi_{n+1}^2] = \mathbb{E}[S_n^2] + 1.$$

The expected squared position climbs by exactly one per step — deterministic bookkeeping ticking away underneath genuine randomness. When this course reaches quadratic variation, \((\mathrm{d}W)^2 = \mathrm{d}t\) will be this same fact wearing continuous clothes.

Third: nothing above used the coin. The proof in §2 used independence and finite variance — full stop. That's why your run does everything twice, once with ±1 steps and once with Gaussian steps: same slopes (0.994 / 0.497 in my validation run), same 0.80 constant. The scaling law is a property of accumulating independent noise, not of any particular noise. This universality is what earns Brownian motion its throne in 2.2.

Step 6 · the punchline

6Why the continuous limit forces √dt

Now the question the page exists for. Fix a horizon — one year, \(T = 1\) — and chop it into steps of length \(\mathrm{d}t\), so there are \(n = T/\mathrm{d}t\) of them. To reach a continuous-time model you must shrink \(\mathrm{d}t \to 0\) while the step size shrinks too. Shrink it as \(\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}\) — which exponent survives? F2 answers by itself:

$$\operatorname{Var}(S_T) = n \cdot \left(\mathrm{d}t^{\alpha}\right)^2 = \frac{T}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathrm{d}t^{2\alpha} = T\,\mathrm{d}t^{\,2\alpha - 1}.$$

Read the exponent: \(\alpha > \tfrac12\) sends the variance to zero — the model freezes into a flat line. \(\alpha < \tfrac12\) sends it to infinity — the model explodes. Only \(\alpha = \tfrac12\) leaves something alive: \(\operatorname{Var}(S_T) = T\), finite, nonzero, and — pleasingly — linear in the horizon, which is §3's variance-adds law surviving into continuous time. √dt isn't a convention someone chose; it's the only exchange rate between step size and step count at which randomness neither dies nor explodes.

One generalisation, and it's the road map for the rest of Part 2. Over \([0,T]\) there are \(T/\mathrm{d}t\) slots. A deterministic per-slot quantity survives summation if it is order \(\mathrm{d}t\) (count × size \(= T\)). A zero-mean, independent random per-slot quantity survives if it is order \(\sqrt{\mathrm{d}t}\) (count × size² \(= T\) in variance — F2 again). Everything else vanishes or blows up. So a continuous-time model of anything has exactly two kinds of term available:

$$\mathrm{d}X = \mu\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma\,\mathrm{d}W$$

(That \(W\) is a glyph borrowed one page early: it names the walk itself in its continuous limit — Brownian motion, 2.2's headline object — and \(\mathrm{d}W\) is its next √dt-sized increment, not a derivative. Nothing new is being used; it's this page's object wearing next page's name.)

— a drift built from the surviving deterministic scale and noise built from the surviving random scale. When you meet this in 2.6 it will not be a modelling choice to justify but the only two survivors of the limit. And the classic implementation bug of 2.6 — writing sigma * Z * dt instead of sigma * Z * sqrt(dt) — is now diagnosable on sight: it picks α = 1 for the noise, the left-hand panel above, and simulates a market whose randomness quietly freezes to nothing as you refine the grid.

Q1

A fair ±1 walk runs 10,000 steps. Typically, how far from zero does it end?

✓
About 100. sd = √10,000 = 100; the mean absolute distance is ≈ 0.8 × 100 = 80. The √n law.
✗
About 0 — the +1s and −1s cancel. zero mean ⇒ zero distance
E[Sₙ] = 0 is exact, but it averages over runs: +80 and −80 cancel in the mean while both sit 80 away. Cancellation within one run is imperfect, and the leftover grows like √n.
✗
About 5,000 — roughly half the steps go uncancelled. linear growth
Distance from the start grows like √n, not n: the fraction of steps surviving cancellation shrinks as 1/√n. After 10,000 steps only ~1% of them show as net distance.
✗
About 10,000 — it took 10,000 steps. displacement = path length
The path walked is always exactly n steps long; displacement is where you ended up. The gap between those two numbers is the whole subject of this page.
Q2

A random walk's typical distance from start after time t is d. After time 4t it is typically:

✓
2d. Variance adds: Var ∝ t, so sd ∝ √t. Four times the variance is twice the sd.
✗
4d. sd adds like variance
It's the variance that quadruples. sd is its square root: √(4t) = 2√t. Standard deviations of independent stretches never add linearly — (√a+√b)² = a+b+2√(ab), which overcounts.
✗
Still about d — extra steps bring extra cancellation. spread saturates
Every independent step adds variance; the spread grows without bound. It only grows slower than time — √t, not t.
✗
16d. variance mistaken for sd
16 = 4² squares the growth a second time. Var goes ×4; sd goes ×2; nothing goes ×16.
Q3

Building a continuous-time model on a grid of step dt over a fixed horizon, the random increment must be sized like:

✓
√dt. n = T/dt steps of size c·√dt give Var(S_T) = c²T — finite and nonzero. The only surviving exponent.
✗
dt, like every other increment in calculus. noise scales like drift
Var = (T/dt)·dt² = T·dt → 0: the randomness freezes to a flat line as the grid refines. This is also 2.6's classic bug, σ·Z·dt.
✗
Any power of dt — it's a normalising convention. √dt is a convention
The pincer table shows α > ½ dying to zero and α < ½ exploding to infinity across four decades of dt. ½ is forced by variance addition, not chosen.
✗
It depends on the step distribution. distribution sets the scaling
Coin and Gaussian steps produce identical slopes (the experiment runs both). Independence and finite variance fix the scaling; the distribution only matters for finer questions than this page asks.
Q4

Two independent sources of daily P&L noise have standard deviations £30k and £40k. The combined daily sd is:

✓
£50k. Variances add: 900 + 1600 = 2500 (£k²), and √2500 = 50. Independent risks combine in quadrature.
✗
£70k. sds add
30 + 40 adds the sds — that's the worst case of perfectly co-moving risks, not independent ones. Independence kills the cross term (§2's proof), leaving √(30² + 40²).
✗
£35k — roughly the average of the two. diversification averages risk away
Adding an independent risk always increases total variance; it can't pull the combined sd below the larger source (£40k). Diversification helps relative to £70k, not relative to £40k.
