The so what

2Three decisions a developer makes with this model

dS = μS dt + σS dW is a recipe for one step: how far S moves over the next dt, given where it is now. Every later page needs prices that move — to hedge against (2.7), to average over (2.8), to integrate against a payoff (2.9) — and a recipe per step is not yet a price. The person who turns the recipe into prices is a developer writing a simulator, and they make three decisions. Each has a wrong answer that produces a plausible-looking number, and the page exists to make the right answer obvious.

1 · Draw or step?
If the payoff only looks at S_T (a European option, 2.9), draw S_T directly from the solution S_T = S₀e^((μ − ½σ²)T + σW_T): one normal per path, exact, no grid. The wrong answer is to leave out the −½σ². §3.
2 · If you step, how many steps?
If the payoff looks at the path — a barrier, an average, or 2.7's hedging simulator, which rebalances on a grid — you cannot draw S_T alone, you step. For GBM you step exactly: the solution holds between any two grid points. Euler, S_{i+1} = S_i(1 + μ dt + σ√dt·Zᵢ), is what you are left with when the model has no solution, and GBM is where its cost can be measured: about 1 % per path at 100 steps, shrinking like 1/√n, mean zero. Averages ignore it; spreads keep it. §4.
3 · How do you know it is right?
Three tests on the simulated paths: realised volatility = σ, mean S_T = S₀e^(μT), median S_T = S₀e^((μ − ½σ²)T). The bug they catch is σZ·dt in the step, which everyone writes once. §5.

The maths the page needs is already done: 2.5's lemma on log S gave d(log S) = (μ − ½σ²) dt + σ dW, and the bridge above turned that into the solution in six rungs. What is new on this page is not a theorem but the three numbers that let a developer act: the −½σ² (decision 1), the √dt error of stepping (decision 2), and the σ√dt signature of the bug (decision 3).

The mean price is held up by the right tail: 57.9 % of paths end below it, and that fraction is Φ(½σ√T) = Φ(0.2), independent of μ. The expected price and the odds for a single path are different things, and the gap between their centres is the drag: nobody "sees" the median, but a single path is as likely to finish below 102 as above it, and 57.9 % likely to finish below the mean.

Decision 2 — C2 and C4, the same normals, two methods

4If you step, know what it costs: 1 % per path at 100 steps

What this decides. A barrier option, an average-price option, and 2.7's hedging simulator all look at S along the way, so S_T alone is not enough: you step, and you choose n. Two things to know before you do. First, GBM never needs an approximate step: rung 4 holds between any two grid points, so S_{i+1} = S_i·e^((μ − ½σ²)dt + σΔWᵢ) is exact, one normal per step, and 2.7 should step that way. Second, the models that come later (local vol, Heston — Part 4) have no such line, and there you step the recipe literally: Euler, S_{i+1} = S_i(1 + μ dt + σ√dt·Zᵢ). That is an approximation, and GBM is the one model where its error can be measured against the truth — which is what this section does. The question is how big the error is, how it shrinks with n, and — the part that matters most — whether it is a bias (which survives averaging) or a noise (which does not).

The number. At 100 steps the Euler price is 1.15 % off the exact one on the same normals, rms over paths, and 3.4 % off on path 1. Each 100× more steps buys 10×: 1.15 %, 0.36 %, 0.11 %, 0.034 %, 0.012 %. That is √dt — Euler–Maruyama's strong order ½ — and it is why your P2's 100× was a square root too optimistic. To bring a per-path quantity to 0.1 % you need 10⁴ steps; to 0.01 %, 10⁶.

The rule: means ignore it, spreads keep it. The last column is the one to remember: the gap's mean is zero on every grid, within its standard error. The stepping error is a zero-mean noise, not a bias. Anything that is a mean over paths — a price — is nearly indifferent to it, because the noise averages out: the 20,000-path Euler run at n = 10⁴ returns a mean of 110.62 ± 0.32 against 110.52 and a median of 101.99 ± 0.36 against 102.02, with the −½σ² emerging on its own though no step contains it. Anything that is a spread over paths — a P&L distribution, a hit-rate on a barrier, the width of anything — keeps it, because a zero-mean noise adds to a variance instead of cancelling out of it. 2.7's simulator looks at the P&L spread across many hedged paths; that spread is where a path-level noise would show up, which is one more reason 2.7 steps GBM exactly.

Why — in three lines, from 2.5

log S^E_T − log S₀ = Σ log(1 + xᵢ) = Σ xᵢ − ½Σ xᵢ² + … , xᵢ = μ dt + σΔWᵢ
Taylor for log along the Euler path — 2.5's (ΔX)²-form with g = log. Σ xᵢ = μT + σW_T exactly; the second term is −½σ²Σ(ΔW)² plus dt-sized pieces
log S^exact_T − log S₀ = μT + σW_T − ½σ²T
the solution: the same line with Σ(ΔW)² replaced by T — 2.3's rulebook, 2.5's dt-form
gap = −½σ²(Σ(ΔW)² − T) + (1/n)
2.5's (ΔW)²-vs-dt noise, with g″b² = (−1/S²)(σS)² = −σ². Σ(ΔW)² − T has mean 0 and sd T√(2/n): hence a noise, hence √dt. Path 1 at n = 100: Σ(ΔW)² = 1.438, −0.08 × 0.438 = −0.0350 against the measured gap −0.0348

Euler is not "approximately the SDE": it is 2.5's lemma with Σ(ΔW)² left as the dice rolled it instead of replaced by T. That is the whole error, and the JSON checks it path by path: the gap minus that one term has rms 20× smaller at n = 100 and 2,000× smaller at n = 10⁶, shrinking like 1/n (results.grids[*].resid_lead, slope −1.001). It is also why the error is √dt for any SDE whose volatility depends on X — the b² in ½b²·∂²f/∂x² multiplies a Σ(ΔW)² that does not know it should be T — and dt-sized when b is a constant.

Decision 3 — C3, the index's spot-the-bug as a unit test

5Test the simulator — three numbers it must reproduce

What this decides. Whether to trust the paths. Three properties of GBM are cheap to measure on simulated paths and sharp enough to fail on the common bugs: the realised volatility of each path must be σ; the mean of S_T must be S₀e^(μT); the median of S_T must be S₀e^((μ − ½σ²)T). The mean and median tests are §3's table, and they catch a missing −½σ² (mean 8 % high) or a −½σ² applied to the mean. The realised-volatility test catches the step itself.

The bug. Write σZ·dt for the kick instead of σZ·√dt. Nothing crashes; the paths look like prices; the mean is still S₀e^(μT). But every kick is √dt times too short — dt is smaller than √dt for every dt below 1, which is where your P3 went the wrong way — so the realised volatility is σ√dt: 0.004 at n = 10⁴ against 0.4, a hundred times too small, and the finer the grid the worse it gets. The spread of S_T collapses by the same factor. A test that asserts realised vol within a few percent of σ fails instantly; nothing else on the page would.

Why √dt is right. 2.1's √dt: n kicks of size σ√dt have total variance n·σ²dt = σ²T whatever n is, because variances add and standard deviations do not. n kicks of size σdt have total variance σ²T·dt, which vanishes as the grid is refined. The correct column reading 0.400 on every grid is the √dt doing exactly that job.

A second tell — the drift stops being negligible

On the correct path the drift per step, μ dt, is tiny next to the kick σ√dt (10⁻⁵ against 4 × 10⁻³ at n = 10⁴). On the buggy path both are dt-sized, so the drift is a quarter of the kick on every step and the path climbs with almost no jitter — the red line in §4's figure. The √dt is what keeps drift and noise on different scales; take it away and they land on the same one. (realised_vol_bug.mean, the sum-of-squares version, reads √(σ² + μ²)·√dt for exactly this reason: 0.0410 against 0.0412 at n = 100.)

  • E[S_T] = S₀ and the median is S₀ too — the drag forgotten. log(S_T/S₀) is Gaussian with mean −½σ²T, not 0, and the median of S_T is the exponential of that: 92.3, not 100.
  • E[S_T] = S₀e^(−½σ²T), below S₀ — the drag applied to the mean. The −½σ² is cancelled in the mean by E[e^(σW_T)] = e^(½σ²T) (2.5's E[e^W] = e^(t/2) with W → σW). It survives only in the median.
  • E[S_T] > S₀ because volatility adds value — the lognormal factor counted twice. With μ = 0 the mean is exactly S₀: 100.02 ± 0.04. Volatility spreads the distribution and lowers its median; it does not raise its mean.
Q2

You simulate S_T = S₀e^(μT + σ√T·Z) — the chain rule's exponent, no −½σ² — and price a European option from 10⁶ draws. The price is:

  • Too high, by about e^(½σ²T) on the underlying — 8 % at σ = 0.4, T = 1 — and more draws make it more precisely wrong — every S_T is multiplied by e^(½σ²T); the mean comes back at 1.083·S₀e^(μT). The drag is not a sampling effect, it is in the exponent.
  • Right: the −½σ² is a correction to the log, not to the price — the log and the price treated as separate. S_T is the exponential of the log-return, so a mean that is ½σ²T too high in the log is e^(½σ²T) too high in the price. Measured: mean log-return 0.0198 against the chain rule's 0.1.
  • Too low, because the drag was left out — the drag's sign read from the median. Leaving out −½σ² makes the exponent bigger, not smaller; the median rises from 102.0 to 110.5 and the mean from 110.5 to 119.7.
  • Right on average, noisy per path — a bias mistaken for a noise. A per-path noise has mean zero and shrinks with more draws; a wrong exponent shifts every draw the same way and 10⁶ draws pin the wrong mean to ±0.05.
Q3

Euler steps and the exact solution are built from the same normals. At n = 100 steps, and then at n = 10,000, how far apart are the two S_T?

  • About 1 % at n = 100, about 0.1 % at n = 10,000 — ten times closer for a hundred times the steps — rms 1.15 % and 0.11 % on 1,000 paths; the gap is ½σ²(Σ(ΔW)² − T) to leading order and shrinks like 1/√n (slope −0.501).
  • They match: same normals, same price — "the SDE is the recipe" taken literally. Euler leaves Σ(ΔW)² as the dice rolled it; the solution has replaced it by T. On path 1 at n = 100, Σ(ΔW)² = 1.44 and the prices differ by 3.4 %.
  • About 0.1 % at n = 100, and a hundred times closer at n = 10,000 — the error assumed to be dt-sized. It is √dt-sized: n terms (ΔW)² − dt, each of sd √2·dt, add to √(2/n)·T. That is 2.3's Σ(ΔW)² noise, and it is 1/√n.
  • About 1 % at both: more steps do not help — Euler mistaken for a biased method. The gap's mean is 0 within s.e. on every grid; it is a noise, and the noise shrinks: 1.15 %, 0.36 %, 0.11 %, 0.034 %, 0.012 % down the five grids.
Q4

Same Euler simulator, n = 100, 10⁵ paths. You read off (a) the mean of S_T — a price — and (b) the spread of S_T across paths. The 1 % per-path stepping error shows up:

  • In (b), hardly at all in (a) — the per-path gap has mean zero on every grid (−0.02 % ± 0.04 % at n = 100), so it cancels out of a mean; a zero-mean noise adds to a variance, so it stays in a spread. 20,000 Euler paths at n = 10⁴ return a mean of 110.62 ± 0.32 against 110.52.
  • In both equally: 1 % is 1 % — a noise mistaken for a bias. A bias survives averaging; this gap changes sign from path to path (the first five paths at n = 100: −3.4 %, −1.5 %, +0.6 %, +1.2 %, −0.02 %) and its mean is zero within s.e.
  • In (a) in full, hardly at all in (b) — mean and spread swapped. An expectation does not see a zero-mean noise; a variance does — it adds the noise's variance to its own. A P&L distribution is a spread, which is why 2.7 steps GBM exactly rather than by Euler.
  • In neither: the same normals give the same answer — the recipe taken for the solution. Same normals, different arithmetic: Euler keeps −½σ²Σ(ΔW)², the solution has −½σ²T, and Σ(ΔW)² ≠ T on a grid (1.44 on path 1 at n = 100).
Q5

A simulator writes the step as S_{i+1} = S_i(1 + μ dt + σZ·dt). At n = 10⁴, which test fails, and how?

  • Realised volatility comes out at σ√dt = 0.004 instead of σ = 0.4 — 100× too small — the kick is σ dt instead of σ√dt, and dt/√dt = √dt = 0.01. The mean of S_T still passes (it is set by μ dt, which is right), so the volatility test is the one that catches it.
  • Realised volatility comes out 100× too big — √dt read as larger than dt. For dt < 1, √dt > dt: √(10⁻⁴) = 10⁻², a hundred times larger. The buggy kick is the smaller one.
  • No test fails: dt and √dt differ only in how the limit is written — the √dt taken as notation. It is the size of a Brownian increment (2.1): n kicks of σ√dt have total variance σ²T whatever n is; n kicks of σ dt have total variance σ²T·dt, which vanishes as n grows.
  • The mean of S_T fails: it comes out far below S₀e^(μT) — volatility and drift confused. The mean is S₀(1 + μ dt)ⁿ either way; the bug leaves it alone and removes the spread. Only the volatility test and the median test (which rises toward the mean as the spread collapses) see it.
